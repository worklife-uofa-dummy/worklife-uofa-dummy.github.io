Shane’s Story

When asked what part of work he most enjoys, Shane answers without hesitation: “When I’m working with the cattle…that’s when I tend not to think of anything else…‘Cause that’s my time.” He then adds a quick qualifier: “Until something goes wrong, and then it becomes work again.”

Shane has spent 14 years as a pasture supervisor with Alberta’s Public Lands department. He is one of only four public employees responsible for managing public pastures in Alberta. Operating on his own, or with a small staff of seasonal workers, Shane is responsible for herding cows (owned by a range of private owners) through the province’s Special Areas, allowing them to graze during the summer months. He is also charged with maintaining the land itself: ensuring adequate drainage, preventing overgrazing, and acting as a liaison with oil well workers and hunters to ensure that they follow the rules and laws governing public land use. Shane takes his responsibility to the land and the animals under his care very seriously. While he tries to avoid talking politics with many of the people he works with –“there’s no need to start a fight or a disagreement over it, because…it’s something you’re not going to win”–he is firm in his own conviction about stewardship:

There is more man-made problems causing our climate crisis than there is livestock. …That's why I feel we have to be, as stewards of this land, be really careful on how we do things…We're part of the problem, and we need to be part of the solution.

Shane’s affinity for horses and cattle developed early. As a young man, working on his parents’ cow-calf ranch in southwest Saskatchewan kept his days busy: “If you weren’t going to school you…were putting up hay, you were seeding. You’re doing fieldwork, you’re calving cows, you’re removing cows.” He remarks on the similarity between working with his family’s herd and the duties of his current job as a pasture supervisor, pointing out that it was “pretty much what we do here…where [you] move cows and calves someplace and then you move them somewhere else. And then at the end of the year, you wean ‘em and ship ‘em.”

Given his lifelong familiarity with the rhythms of ranch work, Shane originally thought that he and his wife would take over his parents’ property once they grew too old to take care of it. He pauses briefly before saying, “That didn’t materialise.” What did materialise was a five year drought across Western Canada. The drought hit southern Saskatchewan especially hard, and by 1987 it had caused severe repercussions in the province’s agricultural and ranching industries. Shane’s parents’ farm quickly found itself struggling. After several years of this adverse climate, “it became economically unviable…to stay there.” Though his parents’ ranch ultimately managed to survive the drought (it still exists today, albeit under new ownership), Shane and his wife made the decision to move to southern Alberta. When asked why they chose Alberta, Shane answers simply that it was because “[t]hat was where the work was.”

Upon arriving in Alberta, Shane found a job in the Waterton Parks area, where he spent several years herding cattle. But it wasn’t long before Shane decided to move on to a new employment opportunity at Deseret Ranches, one of Canada’s largest ranching operations. Shane was drawn to Deseret by the chance to learn new and better ways of managing land and cattle:

It didn’t take long for Shane to pursue his goal of taking on more supervisory responsibility: “I started off as just a cowboy there, and moved up to foreman on one of their units.” As a foreman, he was tasked with a new set of expectations and responsibilities: “I was responsible for kind of the day-to-day operations of the unit that I was in charge of, and assigning daily tasks to the Cowboys that worked for us.” Though now in charge of herding both cowboys and cows, Shane says that he found the task of managing people less challenging than the task of managing cattle:

You know, it was relatively easy. Because everybody there was there because they wanted to be a cowboy. And that was one of the few places where you could be. So everybody that was there was wanting to be there…It was a pretty easy job being a foreman there.

Just like those cowboys, Shane was greatly motivated to be where he was. He describes a strong sense of pride at fulfilling his goal of being a supervisor: “that’s what I was striving [for]. That was my goal…That’s what I was working towards, trying to accomplish. So, I mean, I was happy when I accomplished that.”

This same desire for greater professional and personal development drove Shane to eventually move on from his role as foreman at Deseret. “I had reached a plateau in where I was,” he says. Leaving his former employer in the early 2000s, Shane took on a new position with Public Lands, where he was tasked with supervising the publicly owned pastures near the Wild Horse border crossing.

But just several years later, structural changes to community pasturing up-ended Shane’s experience of work. Changes to public land management in Alberta threatened his newfound security as a public employee:

Public lands privatised their operations of the community pastures. And so then I became a contract worker…[W]hen the board changed, they kind of changed the way they wanted to go…[w]hich forced me to find another job.

Put out by this new privatised approach, Shane became worried about his long term financial prospects in the ranching industry, where there were fewer and fewer jobs offering benefits or pensions. Luckily, Shane’s search ended when he secured his current position as a pasture supervisor for Public Land. This position allowed him to obtain the pension and job security he had been seeking.

At the same time, Shane’s current role has meant increased responsibility and stress. His seasonal herd of cattle has grown exponentially, as more and more private owners have chosen to take advantage of the grazing services provided by his office:

[Community pastures] started back in the ‘50s–late ‘40s, early ‘50s–when there was a lot of small farms and ranches in the area, some place for them to put their cows for the summer. And that was what it was originally started for. And then,...as those small operations kind of disappeared and have gotten bigger, our circle of people we take in has gotten bigger…[O]ur farthest patron is probably two hours away.

In this new landscape, Shane translates the stakes of his job into starkly economic terms: “I am looking after ten million dollars worth of other people’s assets.“ This kind of fiduciary responsibility sometimes weighs heavily on Shane:

It's stressful. So…our goal each year, I mean, we're never going to return 100% of the livestock back. Just, it just isn't going to happen. But as long as we can prevent the preventable deaths, then I'm happy. And most patrons are happy. It's the preventable ones, when we start losing them, then we aren't doing our job.

If there is one thing that Shane is more invested in than his work, it is his family. He and his wife have three daughters, who are now fully grown. Shane says that while he has never had trouble balancing the demands of work and family, he still feels a keen pressure to fulfill his duties at both work and home:

I always have felt and, and will always feel, I mean, this is a huge responsibility. And you have to take the time that it takes to do this job right. It doesn't mean that we don't plan around things, so that we can do stuff with our kids. When things are on, [when the] kids were younger and there’d be a Gymkhana here and there, I mean, we always took that time to go. But it may mean the next day or two you were an hour or two later getting your job done, to catch up.

Moreover, Shane says that one of the reasons that he has worked so hard throughout his career is so that his daughters and grandchildren would have the ability to choose their own path in life, a choice he feels he never really had:

I think an education is very important. I didn't get that opportunity when I was growing up to- I graduated high school, but it was… there was work to be done…And I wanted my kids and grandkids to have the opportunity…It doesn't have to be university, but just… education, a further education in something that they want to do is not a bad thing. It's not a bad investment.

When asked if he ever wanted his daughters to follow in his footsteps in the ranching business, he says that he always thought: “if that’s what they choose, I will fully support them.” Shane always imagined his daughters pursuing alternative career paths, but found plenty of opportunities to share his joy of ranching work with them. He taught them to ride, and took them out with him on his rounds in the pasture. His voice radiates warmth as he reminisces: “[W]e worked together an awful lot. They thought I was crazy…We’d be… riding along, coming back after a long day, singing, there was jokes told, they just thought I was a crazy old man.”

Now, with his daughters fully grown, the challenge of balancing work and family has shifted to securing time with his granddaughters. Even when his work is busy, Shane says he always makes the effort to do right by them: “I have a little granddaughter’s birthday in October, right in the middle of roundup. But…we always get down there for her birthday. It may be at supper time. But we get down there.”

The remoteness of Shane’s work has given him a particular perspective on the COVID pandemic. “We are kind of isolated out here anyway,” he says. “The first little bit going to town was different and scary because you didn’t… really know what to expect.” And while he still takes the threat of COVID seriously, when the subject of pandemics comes up, Shane’s thoughts pivot back to an earlier outbreak. In 2016, six cases of tuberculosis were reported among a herd of cows in Alberta, leaving a lasting impression on Shane. While a Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) investigation failed to find any further infected animals, the months following the initial discovery were some of the most stressful in Shane’s career:

That was a nightmare. That was a nightmare. Because we had 2500 pairs, 40 different owners that I had to, for a five year period, basically show CFIA where those owners’ cattle were for the five months that they were here…Plus, plus, we were trying to get cattle out. Our whole south end…We couldn't get them out. Because everything that [way] was totally quarantined…So we had to build a set of trails on a weekend so we could get cattle out of there.

Through it all, Shane made sure to document everything, sometimes working into the wee hours of the morning so that he would have an update ready for government regulators come sunrise:

[T]hey would phone on like, a Sunday evening at nine o'clock, wanting that information at 7am the next day…My office manager in Hanna... she was getting worried because, I mean, [I’d be up till] three o'clock, four o'clock, five o'clock in the morning, [and] I just sent the email off to her at the same time we did CFIA, and then I would go to bed. She was worrying about me.

While most years are not as hectic as those that followed the tuberculosis outbreak, Shane says that there are plenty of other frustrations to contend with as a public pasture supervisor. In addition to keeping the cattle owners and the government happy, Shane is also the first point of contact for other groups that use the land: oil and gas, [and] hunters. Shane’s experiences of these groups are not always as respectful as he would like: “We have well-checkers, we have… crews in and out…So I have rigs of every shape and size, tearing around and [I’m] trying to keep them under control and not wrecking stuff.” But as difficult as it can sometimes be with the oil and gas crews, Shane finds the hunters even more so:

It seems over the years, they have become less respectful. Where years ago…they were respectful of the grass and kept vehicles on trails. Now they drive all over the place. And they used to let you know they were coming. Now…there’s the move that “this is public land that's our land, we can do whatever we want on it,” even though there are certain rules that they're supposed to follow.

For Shane, preserving the ecological health of the lands under his care is not just a professional obligation, but also a personal one:

Having lived through Saskatchewan’s severe drought in the 1980s, Shane says he harbours a fear for the “unknown” future that will result from the continued effects of anthropogenic climate change:

I'm worried about where this world is going to, the direction it's heading….about the climate change, seeing the turmoil that this world seems to be in. And just, like the pendulum has swung so far to one side…[A]nd usually it takes something real drastic to bring that pendulum back to a happy medium. And I'm afraid of what that drastic change is going to be. Whether it's… just an economic collapse, or what it is, I'm not sure, but I worry about that for my kids and my grandkids.

For now, Shane plans to continue to watch over the land and animals under his care. All in all, he doesn’t see a lot changing in his own work as he moves towards retirement: “[F]ive to 10 years…this will stay pretty consistent.” And though he plans on eventually leaving his job as a pasture supervisor, Shane has no intentions of giving up the work of ranching:

When I retire, then I can go help friends and neighbours and work by the day when I want to work. Do whatever…job I want to do…Basically what I'm doing, but not having the responsibility. I can see just going on as day help, helping somebody gather cows, brand, wean, whatever it is, or move cows.

Retirement would also give Shane more time for his favourite hobby: saddlemaking. While selling the saddles that he makes could provide him with an additional source of income, Shane is careful to maintain a strict demarcation between this personal, artisanal pursuit and his regular work: “I limit it to a hobby, so I have fun. Because if it becomes a business, then I might as well quit [my job] and go do that. I don’t want to have two businesses.”

After retirement, Shane says that he and his wife are also anticipating a change of scenery. They see themselves moving to somewhere in the foothills bordering the Rockies: “I love being on the west side of the mountains so I can see the mountains… And we don’t have that here. And I miss that.” Despite his worries for the future, Shane trusts that his hard work will not go unrewarded:

[Y]ou get out of life what you put into it. The more you're willing to put into it, the more you're going to be able to get out of it. And maybe not right it that time when things seem like you're working like a dog and that, but down the road that will pay dividends.
