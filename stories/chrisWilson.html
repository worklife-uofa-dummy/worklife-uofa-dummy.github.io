Chris’s Story

For business owner Chris Wilson, Birch Mountain Enterprises (BME) is an integral and inseparable piece of his identity. Located a just outside of Fort McKay First Nation, the Northern Alberta community of Chris’s youth, BME is both a commitment to the Nation as well as a testament to the hard work of Chris and others. Having grown the company from a fledgling mechanic and welding enterprise, into a powerhouse trucking and service provider that provides employment and opportunities for people across Canada has been one of Chris’s greatest sources on satisfaction.

When the company was founded in 2005, the Oil Sands industry was a different place. Having cut his teeth as a heavy-duty mechanic for Syncrude, Chris was eager to have something to call his own. Utilizing the skills and connections he’d learnt and inspired by the entrepreneurial spirit of both his own mother and father, Chris took the plunge and founded BME. But founding a company and making sure it is properly navigated through the difficult startup time are two different things, and early on Chris found it was his own willingness to put in crazy hours that set him and his partners apart from the competition.

“We used to have a competition,” he recalls. “Who could outwork the other and work longer hours and work more days in a row. Oh, you worked 27 days this month? I’m going to work 28 days… Looking back oh my god, we were foolish.”

The drive and willingness to learn have always been an integral part of who Chris has been. In high-school Chris dabbled with the idea of planning a career as an environmental conservationist, but the six years of schooling it would take turned him away from the idea, he wanted to get to work right away.

“Six years to become a fish and wildlife officer. I said, that’s too much, I don’t know if I got it in me. I did fairly well in school, but I wanted to work.”

Pivoting away from environmental conservation, Chris instead went to work as a laborer for Fort McKay First Nation. At 17, he had already been a volunteer for the fire department but now he was put in charge of supervising summer students as they built a cabin on the reserve land of Moose Lake. It was a taste of responsibility blended with hard work that Chris remembers well.

“That was the first year that we built the cabin in the Moose Lake area, out of spruce logs. It was a hand-crafted log house from scratch. 18 years old… I had the responsibility of looking after eight other summer students. We had a camp cook, we had what we would call a log house engineer–somebody with experience on building a lot of houses–and we had one medic. We were flown in, dropped in the middle of the woods, and got to spend the summer there camping, you know, cutting trees and notching them, hauling them. We didn't have no mechanical means. So, we had to haul with ropes, it was pretty exciting.”

When the position concluded Chris was offered a role as a general laborer in the oil patch. In August of 1994, Chris started to work in the tool crib of Syncrude handing out equipment and tooling to the shop mechanics. It was a start, but still not enough of a challenge for Chris.

“It took me a while to enjoy being a labourer and [the job was] slower than what I was used to. Like I was a worker, I wanted to cut trees, to haul trees. I wanted to do hard labour where, you know, [instead] I'm handing out tools. I'm sitting on my butt . . . So it was a little bit hard. It took me a couple months to slow down.”

At one point Chris told his supervisor, “I need more, I need to work harder.” Their advice was patience. After a few more months, that patience was rewarded as Syncrude began assigning more roles and responsibilities to Chris and he began manufacturing hydraulic hoses.

“I’m functioning, I’ve got a real chore here. I gotta go out, I got to build a hydraulic hose. I’ve got a real job.”

More opportunity would follow, and Chris would rise to the challenge. Within eighteen months Chris was offered an apprenticeship as a heavy-duty mechanic. The position came with a commitment to four years of schooling and many hours of hands-on learning. He was rotated through Syncrude’s departments: the light and medium duty shops, the truck shop and onto the shovel crew. It was an intimidating trial by fire, but also exciting. “I really found my passion,” said Chris. “Where I wanted to be was on the shovel crew. Working outdoors, any environment. The cold, the weather, the rain – I enjoyed all of them.” After his apprenticeship, Chris continued down his path as a journeyman heavy duty mechanic for another half a decade. He learned more about the electrical and maintenance of the role and put into a leadership position running maintenance shutdowns. “I was leading teams of like 32 skilled trades people at 27, 28 years old.” But throughout all his success at Syncrude, Chris always dreamt of owning his own business.

For years Chris had talked about starting his own business, but then other people would talk him out of it. But by the year 2005 the call had grown too loud to ignore and he made up his mind. He left a secure role at Syncrude and walked into the murky unknown waters of being an entrepreneur.

“I came to the conclusion that I wanted to be my own service technician, service mechanic. I told my Syncrude manager that I’m starting my own business. I’m going to vacate this position. They wished me very good luck [and said] if it didn’t work out for me, “please come back. We value your position here.”

Chris incorporated Birch Mountain Enterprises LTD and went about building the framework of his business. He received a contract servicing heavy haulers and excavators for Fort McKay and continued to grow BME. Within a few months he had convinced his brother Lee, and their friend Ivan leave jobs with Syncrude and join him. With two mechanics, two trucks and a welder they had skills and equipment and were on their way to becoming an established service company. But the job market in 2006 was different, and other companies poached talent and crew away from the small BME team.

“Back in 2006, it was an employee market. So everywhere we were sending our employees–we had up to four mechanics at one point in time, three welders–they would realise that they're a good mechanic. They’d say, what are they paying you? We'll give you $1.50 more or $2 more.”

Where most new business owners would struggle to compete in markets they knew, Chris realized that the early company needed to pivot. It was a lesson that has served him well his entire career. When a gasket blew on his service truck, and a replacement part was six weeks out Chris looked around for something to do while the truck was down. Having bought their own steam truck to wash down equipment, Chris recognized an opportunity. He loaded it up, jumped in and spent the day driving around and handing out business calls. It was a turning point. By that evening he received a call from another company whose own wash bays were down. “I parked my steam truck outside their door and pressure washed all of their equipment.” This ability to adapt to emerging markets and grow the early company was a hallmark of things to come. Three more steam trucks and a growing labor force soon followed and the company expanded into fluid handling.

“Then we got the request, “Oh, can you guys haul water?” Yeah, we could haul water, we'd never turn nothing down. So we rushed down to Edmonton, go pick up a water truck. Come back three days later, we're hauling water. We didn't know how to haul water. But we'd be hauling water.”

Within three short years the business expanded into sewage, maintenance, construction and building ice roads. This early diversification was pivotal to the survival and longevity of the company according to Chris. As the Oil Sands can be a notorious market for boom and bust, Chris saw other companies struggle to survive amid the highs and the lows but for Chris those same highs and lows represented opportunities for expansion. Assembling a world class team, Chris and the BME team would identify new strategic directions.

“You get all hands on deck, and you just kind of sit there and you put an opportunity together, you know, you piece the puzzle together. And then you put it out there.”

As the team would look for new opportunities, Chris’ hands-on approach would demand he continue to work hard and get his hands dirty as well. When expanding into vac-trucks, Chris acquired a Class One license and ran the hydro-vacs to better understand the needs and job conditions he would be sending his drivers into.

“The one thing that I always wanted to do was understand the work that we're doing or where we're putting our employees. So, I ended up doing three, four months of just hands on, get dirty, feel it out, understand the equipment.”

That commitment to being attuned to the needs of each employee, as well as the industry, has rewarded Chris with employees who have been loyal and long tenured with the company. The third and fourth employees of BME are still with the company nearly 20 years after it’s founding. The growth has continued, building the company to a fleet of 200 trucks, nearly 350 employees and major contracts with some of the biggest players in the Oil Sands industry. But growth is not without its challenges.

Since the inception of BME the company has had to survive the boom and bust of the Oil Sands, the 2008 financial crisis, the 2014 Oil downturn and the onslaught of Covid-19. Streamlining the company during the pandemic the company was forced to lay off just under 30 employees, but with smaller crews the team was able to find more efficient ways to complete the same work with less. But as the stresses of the pandemic wound down, the BME crew was confronted with an opposite problem – worker retention.

“I just don't know where everybody went, like two years ago, we always had enough employees. Right now we're shy of where we need to be to be operating efficiently.”</blockquote> Another of the challenges the company now faces is a more aggressive Oil Sands looking for greater margin by squeezing contractors. Although the Oil Sands are doing better than they were before the pandemic, and with greater profits for the large companies, Chris feels that they are finding those profits at the expense of those who help them develop the industry.
But not all changes in industry have been difficult to adapt to for Chris. The shift by the big players in the Oil Sands to take reconciliation with Indigenous communities more seriously is a cause near to his heart. A stout defender of his own local reserve lands, Chris has often put his money where his mouth is, running a youth moose hunting camp and spending weekends out at Buffalo Lake in the winters and summers.
“We need to protect what's left out there. I could drink the water out of the lake [where my cabin is], it's so clean. There's no noise pollution, there's no smoke, there's nothing. Still back to nature. And it was worth the battle. This one here is true and dear to our heart. We have done well with industry in our area, and you know, we don't have much left.”
When approached by an international company to purchase a large equity stake in BME, Chris turned them down and instead worked with his own Nation to purchase a stake in BME.
“[I had] Nothing on my taste buds to sell to an international company. I sat down I looked them in the eye and I said, you know if I was to sell my business, I'd want to sell it to my First Nation. We're all from the Fort McKay First Nation, our hard work would always benefit the community that we're from.”
Chris put together a pitch package and presented it to Chief and Council, and in 2020 Fort McKay Group of Companies took a 51% ownership stake in BME, ensuring that the company would remain local for years to come. With the future of BME in the Fort McKay region secure, Chris has had more time to look back and reflect on the body of work he’s put into his life. The years of wisdom has changed his outlook on a work and life balance significantly since those early days of the company. He is the first to admit that his take charge attitude and long hours are what ultimately fueled BME to launch, but that they were not without cost. Hurt relationships with past partners, and family time was sacrificed, a lesson Chris advises new entrepreneurs not to make.
“If you don't put in the time and effort, you're not going to get much out of this, this effort. So you put your best foot forward . . . It's a hard go for the first three to four years, because it's consistent door knocking, it's consistent handshaking. But don't lose track of your priorities. Your priority is your family. And that's a hard one. That's a hard, hard one to work with when building a business.”
Having been remarried, Chris has begun the process of winding down his involvement with the day-to-day operations of BME. He spends more of his time with his family, confident that the capable team the company has built is able to handle things while he is gone. Three day weekends, vacations, and just unplugging from his workload are all things Chris has become more comfortable with as time has passed. But the pride in what he’s built has stayed with him, and he doesn’t feel the need to fully retire anytime soon. According to Chris, he’s still young and has a lot to give to the community and the region that has given him his life’s work.
