Henri’s Story

After 25 years of running the family business with his wife Lori, Henri is on the cusp of retirement. It’s a scary prospect for a host of reasons. Having worked since he was 15 years old, Henri can’t quite imagine life without it. But more than that, he anticipates missing a specific relationship to work and the workplace: “The main thing is nobody needs you anymore. You know, ‘you can come to work but we don’t need you.’” This sense of impending loss of purpose is heightened by the fact that he and Lori built Bonjour Brioche from the ground up. Through “lots of hard work and a bit of luck” it grew from a shabby building to a destination bakery, and in a neighbourhood that has gone from poor to rich. He points out that back in the 1990s, nobody wanted to be on the east side of Toronto, and now, property sells for millions.

At the same time, Henri feels life is short and is looking forward to finding a more relaxed rhythm.

I wanna get up in the morning and go for a bike ride or walk. Take the dog out. Yeah, no pressure. But I'm [also] afraid I'm gonna miss the pressure. You know, we worked hard for 25 years, and Bonjour Brioche has become something in Toronto, so we are pretty proud and we have great customers. It’s just time to quit. Like a friend just passed, 67, you know, that's not far now [for us]. So let's enjoy it.

Henri can retire with the satisfaction that he has supported many young workers over the years and has passed on his skill and knowledge in artisanal baking. Making a kneading gesture with his hands, he says, “I like my job, I mean, you make something.”

Henri grew up in Lyon, France. “We had a happy childhood, but there was no money. There were three kids living in one room.” Henri’s father, who never had any education, worked in the wine business. His mother had worked as a textile engineer before an injury made it impossible to continue. Turned off by the idea of university, teenage Henri went for his Patisserie Certificat d’Aptitude Professionnelle (CAP) with encouragement from his mother.

I didn’t want to go to university because I saw my brothers studying and I [didn’t] want that, I [wanted] to buy a car. And [so] it was pastry or the army. My mom got me a pastry job, I mean apprentice. That's where I [worked] when I was 15 and I would sleep at the bakery, go home on Sunday after work, stay home on Monday, go back on Tuesdays. And you learn a job. I guess those days [were] too long but you have to do it, you have to work, you have to do something.

The first time Henri came to Canada was in October 1981 at age 20. A family relation in Montreal was looking for a pastry chef, so he did all the paperwork and took the offer. At first he struggled with the language barrier, adjusting to Quebecois and trying to pick up some English, but the chance to settle in was cut short a few years later when he was injured playing soccer. Henri returned to France to recover, and not long after another offer came to work in Canada, this time at a hotel in Toronto. Henri thought sure, I’ll learn English, get my ligament surgery, and then travel the world.

Then Henri “met Lori, and that was it.” He stayed in Toronto to be with her, all the while working various pastry jobs. One day in 1996, Lori said, “What are we doing? Why don’t we do it on our own?” A whirlwind year ensued. Lori spotted a for lease sign while biking down Queen Street, so they looked into it. The place needed lots of renovation and seemed a bit small, and a loan proved difficult, but once a business-savvy friend stepped in to help them negotiate with the bank, “everything changed.” When they opened the bakery later that year, people around them thought they would last three months given how run-down the area was.

Henri and Lori doubled down and within half a year both a growing business and a growing family:

We gave ourselves six months, and if it didn't work, then you have to pay the bills [and move on]. But it took off. It worked. So we had to work hard, like 18 hours. And the funny part is we opened in July and were [also in the process of] adopting a little girl from China. [The agency had said] nine months or even two years to get a baby. But in December, we got a phone call: China’s changing the laws, you have to leave the country by December 30. On January 1, we were in China.

Henri remembers crying when the bus carrying the babies rolled up, and the red dot for good luck on his daughter’s forehead. “And then 24 years later my daughter is going to go to medical school, smart as a whip.”

The early months in the bakery meant “work from seven to seven, take the laundry home, bake the bread [early each morning] downstairs and then go upstairs and make the omelettes, to serve the customers.” They soon called a friend to help upstairs on the retail end and got part-time help for Henri downstairs in the pastry kitchen. Eventually they grew to some 20 part-time employees, with Henri and Lori settling into their respective, complementary roles: “partner in life, partner in work…I make the dough and she puts it away,” he says.

The demand of co-running their own business has sometimes been hard on family, like when Henri works 24 hours straight at Christmas or when Lori has to work a full week and weekend. But for the most part, Henri sees it as a “happy story.” His father had always said that “the richest people are people who work with their hands.” Henri saw his brothers making a lot of money working with their brains and laughed at this idea, but now thinks his father was right. For the most part he was home for dinner every night with his wife and daughter, and he could attend school plays. “So everybody’s got his own. If you’re happy with your life, that’s happiness.” This goes along with leading a simpler life as he gets older. When he was diagnosed with stress-related diabetes at age 45, Henri tried to eat out less and focus on things that mattered closer to home.

Henri also likes to keep things simple when it comes to baking. The menu at Bonjour Brioche hasn’t changed much in over 25 years, but what matters, says Henri, is that it’s high quality. He feels the business was probably successful because “I kept that; bread is bread.” His portrait from 2006 is a reminder that he is “still doing exactly the same move, croissant. Same kitchen.” Henri feels Europeans have a higher appreciation for this basic high quality artisanal baking, and that there is a stronger sense of sociality around good food and bread. Bonjour Brioche has its loyal and appreciative customers, but too many people in Canada “grew up on Wonder Bread” and think that new technologies can make them into chefs.

Henri is astounded that in Paris you can now get a baguette or a pizza out of a machine. At Bonjour Brioche they cut baguettes by hand. “Pretty soon your iPhone’s gonna bake you a baguette. I won’t be that.” He recognizes that this is just normal for his daughter’s generation, who do their shopping online, but laments the impatient and impersonal aspects of fast, virtual communication.

Henri and Lori had already put Bonjour Brioche up for sale when COVID-19 hit. In fact, they had already signed off on a sale when “the guys backed out” because of the new uncertainty of COVID. This experience caused an emotional setback in their road to retirement, but Henri is sanguine about the money they lost:

We’re not obsessed with money. C’est la vie, what are we going to do? . . . Business has been great to us for 24 years, we never had a bad year. Well, except the last two, but we just had to adapt.

Adapting meant having to cut staff, cut their operating hours, and open a take-out window. The window turned out to be a great idea. “People love it. ‘Oh, I got my stroller’. ‘I have my dog.’ It worked, and we’re gonna leave it like that.”

This adjustment was only possible because Henri and Lori already owned their building and were fully equipped. They watched other restaurants and bakeries languish as supply chains withered, or close altogether as they couldn’t make rent. “If we had to pay rent, we’d be closed. We took a loss but we still have people working, and customers are happy.”

Henri sees this as yet another stroke of luck. Henri often circles back around to how lucky he has been, wondering what he might have done in a past life to deserve it. Thinking back to where Bonjour Brioche started, and where it has come, he says,

I always say in life you needed 10 percent luck. Like, we live 10 minutes away. We didn’t want to take the subway, streetcar, drive, it was the perfect spot. We just wanted a mom and pop operation. Like we never envisioned to make it-- So yeah, I guess call it luck, Lori rode her bike at the right time. Or, you know, the guys liked us, gave us a lease.

The other 90 percent is hard work, and after several years of thinking about retirement, the COVID pandemic has helped Henri ask himself, “Why am I working so hard?” At the bakery he has always worked with young people, which has, in turn, helped keep his mind young. But he is feeling it physically. “You pick up a bag of flour [and think] ‘are they heavier now?’. . . You feel it now, going up the stairs.”

Henri looks forward to retirement—taking a vacation without having to take phone calls about issues at work, for example—but still can’t quite imagine relinquishing Bonjour Brioche. After all the time and labor and care that he and Lori have invested, he doesn’t want to sell to just anybody. Sure, if you sell to Starbucks, “You get a cheque, boom boom . . . But you sell with somebody smaller and you see, it’s still yours . . . I want my picture on the wall. I want my table and free coffee. Yeah, it will be hard.”

You don’t even have to go to the bank.” And he knows that he can retire with a sense of satisfaction about his working life:

It’s mine. We made it, me and Lori, it’s ours, it’s Bonjour Brioche, it’s ours, nobody else did it . . . I would do it again. I mean, you don’t know anything else, you don't know if you will succeed. I’m proud of what I did, happy with it.
