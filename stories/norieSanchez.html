Norie’s Story

Taking a moment to step away from the busy unpredictability of her work as the manager of a small dry cleaning business in Fort McMurray, Norie thinks back to her decision to come to Canada. “We heard a lot of stories,” Norrie says. “There were few of our neighbours who lives here, and we saw…that they have a good life here in Canada. It’s very inspiring, you know, like, oh my gosh, how I wish we could go there, too!” Making a living has always been Norie’s primary drive for working. An immigrant from the Philippines, one of Norie’s biggest motivations for moving to Canada was her desire to build a future for her family. For her, earning a wage means being able to save up for things like a house, a car, and her daughter’s college fund. Like many transnational workers, Norie has built towards the goal of financial security through mobility.

Though she loves her home country, Norie says that trying to build a career in the Philippines as a young worker posed a series of unique challenges.

Philippines is a beautiful place. Lots of, you know, the nature, and lots of opportunity also. But it's, I feel like…the opportunity is way better here. Because in Philippines, like, even if you [have a] college degree, when you're going to apply for the job, there’s a lot of requirements that you need to meet, like experience. And under requirements, there is like, along with pleasing personality, there's age bracket and everything. So the competition, you know, it's very competitive.

Norie’s journey to Canada, and to her and her family’s current state of financial security, certainly had its fair share of setbacks. Between navigating immigration laws, enduring long distance separation from family, and even having to pick up the pieces after a devastating wildfire, she and her family have had to face a plethora of obstacles. But through it all, Norie says she has remained determined. “Maybe because I have experience in Philippines,” Norie muses, “my fighting spirit’s so strong!”

As a young adult back in Manila, Norie’s first full time job was working for a party venue. The lion’s share of the business came from hosting kids’ birthday parties, supplemented with more formal bookings such as weddings. During her time there, she worked her way up to a management role. But after five years of employment, she found herself presented with a sudden opportunity: “I have a relative working in Hong Kong. And she called me one day, like, ‘oh, maybe you want to work here in Hong Kong?…because you can go to Canada. This is your stepping stone to go to Canada.’” Due to the difficulty in emigrating directly from the Philippines, taking a short interval as a caretaker in the former British colony would set the stage to allow her to apply for a work permit in Canada.

Norie was enticed by the idea, but it still took three calls from her relative to finally convince her to give up her job. After half a decade working with the same company, she had formed a close attachment to the job and her employers: “I’m just, ‘oh my God, how can I talk to my employer, that I have to resign?’ Like, it’s been five years…I’m working with them. And then the relationship with your bosses, your boss. And our relationship is like, we’re like family.”

Norie says that an even more difficult conversation awaited her at home. Her husband, who previously had had a less than ideal experience working overseas in Korea, was reluctant for her to leave. Moving away from the Philippines would mean not only leaving her husband behind, but also the couple’s school-age child: “He said, ‘look at your daughter…do you think you can, like, [go] too far away from us like that?’” After much discussion, the couple arrived at a compromise: If Norie was still stuck working in Hong Kong after two years, she would come back home. Norie says her husband also eventually came around to being enthused by the promise of moving to Canada: “He’s, like, so excited also. Like when I explained to him like we’re gonna live in Canada, we will have a good life in Canada, we can buy our own car.”

It didn’t take long for Norie to obtain a job offer in Hong Kong. She was hired on as a domestic helper with a family in Central, the district that serves as the beating heart of the island’s financial industry. There, she worked as hard as any stock trader:

When you're in Hong Kong as a domestic helper, like, you will do everything. Like you have to wake up six o'clock in the morning until 11 o'clock in the evening. You're working…Take care of the kids, do everything in the house, the household chores– it's all your responsibility…Even put socks on your employer.

Sticking with her initial plan, Norie only worked in Hong Kong for a year before applying to several hiring services that were seeking live-in caregivers for Canadian households. Within a month, she had gotten an interview, and three days later, a contract. Though her Hong Kong employer stipulated a two-year contract, and they even offered a significant increase in her salary to stay on, Norie made the decision to break her contract in favour of the new opportunity in Canada.

In March 2012, Norie made her way to her new job in Fort McMurray. At first, she found that the small, northern Albertan town did not match her vision of what Canada would be: “When I arrived here, the first thing in my mind is, ‘Canada is a big, big city!’ I did not expect that Fort McMurray, that I’m so excited for, is like, so small. Like, where’s the mall?” Though her job as a caregiver was superficially similar to her work as a domestic helper in Hong Kong, Norie says her experience was very different: “Caregiver here is very easy, because, for me, my employer–my employers–they’re really nice. Like what you see in the contract, they follow everything. And they treat me like a family.”

Though the positive relationship with her employers made the work feel less demanding than at her previous job, as Norie anticipated, the hardest part of her time abroad was being apart from her husband and children. During her off hours, she says that she would spend hours on the phone speaking to her family back in the Philippines:

My employer, I know, she's thinking, like, “Oh, Norie, are you okay? You're always up in your room.” I never go, like after work, like for a walk or for everything, or to hang out with my friends. No, I stay in my room for five hour, six hours calls, video calls, for my family. That's every day until I got my open work permit.

Norie would also make the effort to fly home at least once a year to see her family in person: “I’m so very lucky, because my employer here after one year, he let me take my vacation…My employer here paid for my ticket. And while I’m on my vacation, he kept paying me my regular salary.” The ability to maintain regular contact with her family helped make the separation for work easier to bear.

Over the next four years working as a caregiver, Norie would send the majority of her salary home to her family in anticipation of bringing them over to Canada. She obtained her open work permit in 2014, which allowed her to also take on a second job working part-time at a dry cleaning service in Fort McMurray, the same dry cleaning service that she now manages. Though her contract as a nanny had only been for two years of work, she had promised her employers that she would continue in her role as long as they still needed her. Working two jobs also allowed her to accumulate more savings. Then in 2016, Norie was finally able to gain her permanent residency and send for her family. She says that her employers shared in her joy at finally being able to permanently reunite with her husband and children. She recalls the warmth of that moment: “My family is coming, we’re both crying, but my employer is happy. They’re happy that I–they told me like, we know we need to let go of you here now.” Even now, years later, Norie says she still maintains a relationship with them. Her daughter sometimes babysits their kids, and they occasionally find time to text and visit.

Although Norie had achieved her goal of becoming a permanent resident and saving up enough money to bring her husband and kids to Canada, 2016 proved to be a difficult year for the family. That year, a devastating wildfire in and around Fort McMurray displaced thousands from their homes, Norie among them. When her family landed in Edmonton, evacuation orders for Fort McMurray were still in effect. As a result, the newly reunited family’s first home in Canada was a hotel room in Bonnyville.

When they were finally given the all clear, Norie returned to Fort McMurray to find that the house that she had been staying in had burned down. Norie was devastated: “I prepared everything for my husband, my kids. Like I have a new TV, I have like, I bought some stuff for them, and it’s all gone.” Norie and her family managed to find shelter with a close friend who was also hosting several other displaced families. But though she counted herself lucky to be with her loved ones once again, the experience of life after the fires was a far cry from how they and Norie had imagined their shared life in Canada would begin:

We're two families in the upper level of the house and two families in the basement… So we're all together in the kitchen, in the living room, share for everyone…My family [is] like, “oh my gosh, is this the life that you said?”

Their Canadian dream had always been to have their own house: “Living with other families in the house is like, we’re good. Like, the relationship is good, but it’s not comfortable.” She explains, “you want a privacy. Your dreams is to have a good life here.”

Both Norie and her husband were forced to work even harder to rebuild what they had lost. In addition to her job at the dry cleaning service, where she had transitioned to working full time in 2015, she also picked up evening shifts at Save On Foods as a cashier. Her husband also worked two jobs, alternating every day between hotel and restaurant work. Ongoing restoration of fire and smoke damaged buildings also meant an explosion of business at the dry cleaner. Norie says that there was so much laundry that even the basement and offices became filled with clothes.

In addition to taking care of their kids, this hectic work schedule left them with little free time. Still, in the end, all of that hard work and sacrifice allowed them to realise their dream. A year later, in 2017, they had finally saved up enough money to buy a house.

In the years since, the family has built a comfortable life in their new home country. After the last manager quit during COVID, Norie settled into the leadership seat at the dry cleaning service, taking over the day to day management of the business. As a result of both the stresses of the pandemic and the waning of the oil boom, Norie is not only responsible for reports and paperwork, but also finds herself doing a little of everything. She often works alongside her employees doing the pressing and other tasks necessary to run the cleaners. Unlike the other major dry cleaner in town, Norie’s business processes everything on site, rather than sending larger orders to other processing facilities further away.

Many of the dry cleaner’s other employees are also Filipina. One of them, like Norie, had also worked as a nanny previously in order to secure a work permit in Canada. Another had obtained her entry into the country by way of factory work in Taiwan.

Norrie’s office occupies a small room in the downstairs portion of the shop, across from the tailor station. With so much of the first floor workspace given over to the active processes of cleaning, the office sometimes finds itself doubling as an impromptu storage room. She takes a moment to rearrange the cluttered space so that there is room to sit. One of the shifted objects is a display box containing a wedding dress bust, enclosed in cellophane. Norie explains how developments in the dry cleaning industry now allow cleaners to preserve these kinds of garments in mint condition for decades. It’s become a popular service.

She has to pause her story briefly as one of her employees approaches with a minor problem involving a customer’s payment. Norie delegates the problem quickly and efficiently:

Some clients, before they give the stuff [they say,] “oh, I’m a friend of your boss, like, it's okay!” Oh, I'm happy to hear that you’re friend of my boss, but it doesn't mean that they give you 50% discount on everything. We don't have promo right now. So you have to, if you're really a friend, like, if you're really a friend of mine, you will support my business, right?

While she says that her job comes with its fair share of demanding customers and other problems, she never lets it bother her too much. Norie says that one of the things she enjoys about her work is the people that she works with. She also enjoys that the pace of work keeps her busy.

This spirit of perseverance was especially put to the test during the COVID pandemic. Like many other businesses in the service industry, navigating the sudden demands of COVID measures and public health directives put a lot of strain on Norie and her coworkers. They were forced to take a month off of work, and several employees were laid off as business slowed. Norie says that the pressures of the pandemic caused her to become worried for her family’s financial security:

As a result of the increased cost of living, Norie and her husband also work three nights a week cleaning office spaces in town. She says that this part-time work adds another hour and half onto her nine-hour workday. Still, despite the long hours she keeps, Norie says she’s grateful that her family was able to weather the crisis: “We’re happy because we can manage, you know. That even though it’s pandemic we still managed to pay our bills, our everything, and some savings is still intact.”

After spending a decade working in Canada, Norie can’t help but compare the differences between working in the two countries:

We really need to work here, though. Because, like, compared to Philippines, even if you don't have work, you live, you will survive, you have food. Because like, even you don't have a regular work in Philippines, it's very easy to plant some rice, or, you know, the ocean is there, you can catch a fish whenever you want to eat, like that. But even compare here…Because in Philippines, if you just, if you go to Manila, work for hotel or for the restaurant, your salary will be your expenses also. And maybe not enough for all your expenses…You can’t buy our wants, you know, you don't have extras for that.

She says that working in Canada has given her and her husband the opportunity to save and invest in a future for her and her children. Norie believes that as long as she is willing to work hard, she’ll never have to worry about finding work: “That’s why I love it here, still; as long as you’re hard working. So just don’t be lazy. So you will survive here and you will have a good life here.”

Norie’s plans for the next decade include preparing for retirement and a return to the Philippines: “I don’t want to work and work for the rest of my life, right? Because we–I just want to enjoy our life, too.” Over the years the family has built up assets in the Philippines which include a rice field and a coconut farm. On top of their savings, these investments will allow Norie and her husband to live in relative comfort in the last part of their lives. They plan to give their house in Fort McMurray to their now adult children, either to live in or sell. After working so hard to carve out a new life halfway around the world, Norie is proud of what she has achieved: “You know that Filipino is hardworking…And we’re not complaining…we don’t care as long as we’re earning, like, in a good way. Like even we’re a cleaner or what? It’s okay with us.”
