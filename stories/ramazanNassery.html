Ramazan’s Story

Ramazan Nassery 40

As a young child growing up in Afghanistan, work was presented to Ramazan as a fundamental fact of life. “I think what we grew up with, [as children was], ‘we need to work.’ I think that’s given to our minds.” For much of his life, long hours and busy days were something that Ramazan had little choice in. After leaving Afghanistan as a teenager and then making his way to Canada through a convoluted and arduous route to asylum, work was fundamental to supporting himself and his family and to pursuing his desire to educate and improve himself.

Now, Ramazan owns his own business as an immigration consultant in Fort McMurray, Alberta, helping other emigrants and their relatives move to Canada. While he still espouses the value of hard work, his experiences have led to some unlearning of the expectations he held around the place of work in the broader context of his life. Now, he finds himself wondering what would have happened if, as a young man, he had thought to ask, “why?”

We didn't ask why, we didn't have that, you know, what's called “wisdom” at that time, right? Then you're already in the work, and then you do not ask yourself, “[is it just] because that's what you do?” Is it a sufficient answer or not? You want to get on a plane because your parents, your friends, and relatives told you?...At that time, it didn't come to your mind why you ended up on the plane? Because you should have asked [the] question, before we got into this: “Is it the right answer?”

Ramazan was raised in a rural farming village. One of only a handful of children in his community, even at an early age he felt a strong desire to leave home. “I insisted I wanted to go,” he says. “The country and the situation was as terrible as it is now…My plan was to get out.” In March of 1995, 14-year-old Ramazan convinced his father to send him to Pakistan to pursue an education. Over the course of six years there, he split his days between studying full time and working. “It was a group of us [Afghans] in Pakistan,” he recalls.

We learned English and computers. English class,…many people attended, [but] they could not pass it. They completed level 2,3,4 and then they cannot continue after that. Variety of reasons. But we are some of those exceptional, let's say “talents,” at that time, that we were able to complete the full program of English language. And also study computers.

During the first period of Taliban governance in Afghanistan, it became impossible for Ramazan to return home. As a non-citizen of Pakistan, Ramazan was forced to move out of the country. “In June 2000, [we were] moved to Indonesia. And then, on October [3rd], we moved towards Australia and we stayed . . . on the sea until October 19.” Ramazan and the other refugees were turned away from the Australian border and sent back to Indonesia. When asked how he is able to remember these dates so specifically, he says it is “because these are horrific experiences. I will never forget that.”

Ramazan found transitioning to life in Indonesia difficult. In Pakistan, his education allowed him to have a certain social status, despite being a foreigner. As a migrant to Indonesia, he no longer occupied the same position: “Undocumented, illegal, asylum seekers in Indonesia, we didn’t know what’s gonna happen tomorrow. I totally lost everything. Except, the [English] language and computer skills helped me to grow up again.” Ramazan was able to put the education he received in Pakistan to good use, earning a modest salary teaching his fellow refugees English. Teaching six classes of up to 140 students kept him busy from 8 AM to 11 PM every day, and he says that he found satisfaction in the way his work helped others:

We had 245 refugees. 95% of them, they didn't know what to do. And you're just trying to pass the day. For me, it was busy…But I really enjoyed that…It was really, really good times in one sense for me, [because] I did help them, because they learned the basic English language to communicate and just interact with other people. Most of them moved to Australia and Canada. So that learning opportunity was really helpful for them so that they could come here and function, at least to start.

Meanwhile, Ramazan had started to apply for refugee status with UNHCR in Indonesia. Over the course of several years, he navigated one systemic hurdle after another. Each interview he was given lasted for two to three hours, and after waiting almost a year to find out if his application was accepted or rejected. In one instance, he was rejected because his interpreter in the interview had misidentified him as being from Pakistan. Ramazan didn’t find out about the mistake until two years later, and the lack of follow-up from UNHC in Indonesia meant that he had no chance to set the record straight. Ramazan says such issues are common in the asylum process and finds that his own experiences and frustration with the process have allowed him to understand the similar plights of many of his clients here in Canada with IRCC: “Most people are forced to return from Indonesia to their home country of Afghanistan because they didn’t pass the interview. I don’t know where they end [up]. The sad thing, the sorrow, is that even now in 2024, we experienced the same persecution by Taliban as we did in late 1990s in Afghanistan.”

In June 2004, after his third interview, Ramazan’s application was finally accepted, and he was recognized as a convention refugee by UNHCR. That is when UNHCR transferred his file to Canadian Embassy in Jakarta Indonesia where it was processed and after passing two separate interviews he was finally issued a permanent resident visa. He then got on a flight to Toronto. Though Ramazan had achieved his goal of making it to Canada, he found his experience as a new resident as disorientating, and disheartening, as his move to Indonesia:

Initially, Ramazan expected Canada to be filled with opportunities for work. Such expectations were dashed when he found that the interpersonal skills he had developed to find work in Indonesia did not translate to applying for jobs in his new home of Toronto. “[I thought,] ‘I’ll help this family, or I will help that gentleman.’ Because that is the way I did with them in Indonesia,” Ramazan explains.

I could not find my way. I ended up working as a cashier in one of the grocery stores for the first eight months. That was the most difficult experience of my life. Because I had dreams of Canada in my head. Hopes, and I had the– what is it called?–”plans” to do. But even I could not find the simplest things. After eight months, I was exhausted. It's not because of work. It was because emotionally I was exhausted because, I don't know, this type of work, it didn't fit my goals and my dreams that I had. I haven't worked in that position. I was definitely a refugee [before], but I worked as a teacher in an educational system. I didn't work, you know, labour jobs.

Those first few months in Canada felt like a step backwards to Ramazan.

Sponsored by a government program, Ramazan received modest financial support as well as access to work and educational opportunities. He took out a loan and studied in the community worker program at a local college in Toronto. For his practicum, he volunteered at a settlement services organisation and then was hired as a summer student. But continuing at the organisation proved difficult. As a Hazara minority, Ramazan came up against mistreatment by one of the staff people who was also from Afghanistan. “I have somebody who’s a manager from a different ethnic group that doesn’t want me to be there . . . a sickness that haunts us all the time.” In addition, Ramazan was frustrated that the organization hesitated to hire him in a regular position, failing to recognize what his college professor had seen in him: “[My] professor was saying, ‘Your situation is much different than many of my students because you have such extensive experience in this field.’”

Between being unable to secure full time employment and being made to feel unwelcome, Ramazan made plans to move once again. Initially, Ramazan through Calgary would prove a good destination, given the booming economy and ample jobs. Friends who had struggled in Toronto but had then “found jobs the next day” after moving to Calgary encouraged him to come. In 2006, he made the move, but when he applied as a student to Mount Royal College, he found out he would have to wait a year. Discouraged, Ramazan moved back to Toronto to finish his college program.

In 2008, he once again moved west, where a posting in a newcomer center in Calgary caught his eye: a full-time position with the Immigrant Settlement Services office of the YMCA in Fort McMurray. It was a special pilot program to support workers in the Temporary Foreign Worker Program (TFWP).

Ramazan says that he had little notion about what the northern Alberta city would be like before he actually arrived there. He gives his characteristic playful laugh as he recalls his initial impressions:

The [mental] picture I had is a camp, a really cold camp. There's nobody except some workers. That was the image that I had. But the job posting, the job description, that's what I was interested in. Plus the income…When I talk to my callers when they [first arrive] in Canada, they hear the name of Alberta, and they have a picture of oil sands. And when I came here, there are houses, towns, and people. People actually live in the house. Surprise! This is nice. I purchased this thick blanket…and I have never used it for the first 16 years.

The job itself was initially a challenge, in part because the Temporary Foreign Worker Program was, as he puts it, “new for everybody . . . in Alberta, in Canada. Right? Nobody had any idea how it works!” Still, it was in this position that Ramazan finally found work that was fulfilling. “Attending the entire office environment was pleasant for me,” he explains. “Talking to people, I enjoyed that. Plus, I think I had a lot of things to offer, not only in my position in my workplace, [but also] with the rest of different departments.”

Notably, Ramazan could activate the skills that he had been learning since he was a teenager in Pakistan. His accumulated life experience translated to the circumstances of his job as a settlement worker, where the struggles of his past allowed him to connect with the difficulties of his clients:

I have been through those difficult days and years in stages. I think it allowed me to connect with newcomers to Canada who came to YMCA to receive settlement services, to read them. And I had many, many situations that nobody could deal with…I just went in to talk to them. Though I didn't do anything. It's [just] talking to them or listening to them?... I think those things came because of the type of experiences that I went through. I don't know. It's not the language talking, it is the body language, maybe, facial expressions or something like that. So yeah, those things I was able to do. It was satisfying for me.

Because of the limited scope of the pilot TFW outreach program that Ramazan was hired to coordinate, he couldn’t count on his work to provide him with the stability he wanted. “We have funding for one year, then after that,…maybe we have funding, maybe we do not have funding.” He found it hard to manage his stress on the job while labouring under the uncertainty of what the next year would bring.

Looking for a more solid opportunity, he went to his superiors, asking them “how they expected a professional person to be prone to remain professional with this type of uncertainty in their life?’ Just killing people in energy and in ambition.” Advocating for himself, he obtained a permanent position within the YMCA Immigrant Settlement Services in 2011, which allowed him to then move up to a supervisory role.

Part of the motivation for seeking advancement was the imminent arrival of his new wife from Afghanistan in 2013. After the birth of their first and then second child, “that’s where the challenge started.” A new pressure entered into Ramazan’s life as he tried to navigate his professional and familial obligations. Remembering the exhaustion, he remarks that it was hard, “especially when I had one child, and then we had a second, but then it became really difficult.”

The bureaucratic structure of his job made it difficult for Ramazan to respond quickly whenever his wife and children needed support:

Because, when I need urgently to go to home for half an hour,.. I was looking for the director to get permission to go but was not able to find the person. If I send an email, I receive a response the next day. But I need it today, this afternoon!...I had vacation times, I had all those times, unused. But I couldn't use it the time that I did need it. That was the challenge.

Increasing tension between the need to take care of his family and the demands of his work eventually forced him to leave his job with Immigrant Settlement Services. It was a difficult decision for Ramazan. “I really liked it until a little bit of extra push came from the family responsibility and from restrictions in the workplace…But that was a really difficult decision. I think I was really attached to that position and to the environment.” He compares his work with the organisation to a “newborn baby that I nourished and cherished.”

The experience of having to manage the compounding stressors of work and family imbued Ramazan with an appreciation for the necessity of self-care. With so much of his professional life caught up in providing services for others, for Ramazan, self-care meant cultivating a healthy amount of selfishness. “I realised that I have to be [selfish] because I think that’s something missing in my life.” Learning to put himself first meant pushing against the internalised expectations that he grew up with. “Traditionally, in our culture, [it’s] ‘you are selfish, you are such a bad person.’ You will hear, even here, right now, ‘Oh, he is so selfish.’ It’s not bad. It’s entirely right. You have to be selfish to a certain level.”

At the same time, Ramazan was cautious about being too indulgent of his own wants:

Lots of people become selfish too much that it costs them, they feel different. They use too much resources to mess things up…and then nobody [trusts] them, and then the person is nowhere to be seen. But for me, I think I was lacking sufficient level of selfishness. So, I should add a little bit more of it.

Striking this balance between putting his needs first and his commitment to supporting others led Ramazan to establish his own immigration consultation business in 2015. Moving from an NGO to a private business required him to reorient his view on the work of assisting others in immigrating. “I think one transition was the mindset. Because I come out of a whole life of nonprofit professional capacity to a business model…Something that I realized is, [this is] one of the barriers for me, because I love to do and help people for free.”

In the beginning, Ramazan found himself speaking with clients long past their appointment time slots, and would sometimes give them too much leeway in settling their billing. It took a few months of learning and playing catchup before the business stabilised. Again, it came down to balancing his desire to help others with his own needs. “I can help them to some extent, that I can do, but not at the cost of losing my business and income.” Word of mouth carried many clients to Ramazan, and his business steadily grew.

Outside of the fiduciary responsibilities of running a business, Ramazan does not feel like his view has changed much from his time working in the non-profit sector. “I think I feel the same, but I feel some restrictions,” he says. “Because the mindset [hasn’t shifted]. I approach people through supporting them honestly, right? Truly helping them.” Even so, he realises that switching to a profit model does make some people question his charitable intentions.

One of Ramazan’s only real fears is the possibility of being forced into a position where it feels like compromising his ethics is the only way to keep things afloat. During his time working in the immigration industry, he has seen others cross this line in the name of profit, and it unnerves him.

[One of the] the scary things that often happens is that [new emigres find] either consultants, or lawyers, or ghost consultants and they are betrayed. I think the mindset is important: [are] you providing professional services to get money or to help somebody? That makes the difference in terms of your work quality. Like, if you have the capacity to help somebody, your main priority is the quality of work. It is different than if the priority is the income. So, if income is priority, and work is secondary…there is something that will happen to the quality of work. But if…the importance is the professional service that you'll give, then the income will come by itself. I used that approach, and that's how I have been okay so far. But some people use the other way around.

The onset of COVID had mixed implications for Ramazan’s concerns about the viability of his business. On one hand, the experience reinforced for Ramazan that much of his work could be done electronically. He found the switch to online work remarkable, and relatively smooth. “This online thing works. Because COVID proved to us that you’re not bound to geographic areas. You can reach all over the place. So if you’re not bound, I’m able to do something across the globe wide spectrum.”

At the same time, the introduction of travel restrictions left many of his clients in bureaucratic limbo. While he could continue communications with them electronically, actually getting them or their relatives to Canada proved difficult under the unclear and changing immigration and travel restrictions imposed by the federal government. “The issue is this convention refugee document,” Ramazan explains.

Lots of Canadians, especially those who are soldiers who went to Afghanistan, they know lots of people that they want to sponsor. But this convention, if your document is not there, government doesn't allow them. But we had a zoom meeting with the immigration minister on September 9. And one of the things [raised] was, “okay, can you please remove the condition of having convention refugee documents or to allow Afghanistan citizens, Afghanistan Canadians and Canadians, to come together and sponsor them”...But so far, it hasn’t materialised.

In the coming years, Ramazan plans to expand his business further. In addition to developing his consulting practice, he hopes to open businesses covering areas other than immigration. In particular, he finds himself fascinated with the movement of goods, importing and exporting.

When asked why he works, Ramazan thinks back to his time in Indonesia:

At one point, I was doing exhausting, double shift work with refugee camps. There [was a journalist] from Australia. I told her that “I'm pretty exhausted, I just need to get a break. Can't exist, you know, tolerate anything. It's really tiring for me.” And she told me one thing. She said, “It’s better to be somebody than nobody.” And that really clicked.
