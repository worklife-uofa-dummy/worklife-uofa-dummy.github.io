Neill’s Story

Neill sits in the office, his thoughts drifting back to his struggles on the family farm:

We got hailed out in, I think it was ‘97 or ‘98… And then we got a six figure cheque for hail insurance, you know, crop insurance… [And then] it come time to do tax, and tax man says you now owe me $70,000. So then we had to incorporate the farm, and it did save us some money, but not a whole lot. We ended up paying our bills off. But that was my retirement.

He finishes his story with an ironic chuckle. Growing up as part of the 3rd generation of a farming family in rural Saskatchewan, Neill expected that he would be following in the footsteps of his parents. But then came a series of setbacks, including the fateful hailstorm in the late-90s. While Neill has tried his hand at a variety of different careers, the time he spent trying to save the family farm is something that lingers prominently in the retelling of his history of work: “As I look back we had so many, so many negative things that happened.”

Neill had little choice but to look for work outside of agriculture. Currently, he is employed at the United Farmers of Alberta (UFA), where he is charged with driving and operating a fuel truck that transports diesel and gas to small businesses and agricultural operations. What he most enjoys about the job is interacting with the farmers that make up the majority of the UFA’s clientele: “I can relate to pastures, like how many wires are on the fence? Just certain things that I grew up with.”

Now entering his 5th decade in the paid workforce, Neill says his experience of work, both on the farm and off of it, has been characterised by a need to survive and support: “Everybody has bills, everybody needs food, needs to provide…Everybody is expected to work.” Neill recounts an adolescence spent helping his parents on the family farm:

I was the oldest of four, we lived in a little four-room house. Kitchen and living room and two little bedrooms. 1964 was when my parents built a house. Nice house, a bungalow, farm bungalow. Anyway, at the same time, my dad was busy farming, and my mom was wanting to plant trees, and so it was the oldest of the four kids who gets to haul water and all that.

Though he spent his early years undertaking the necessary labour of chores around the farm, Neill says that his first regular paid job was at a factory: “[It] would have been August 4th of 1969. Right out of high school.” Neil had planned on agricultural school, but a season of bad weather meant that his educational plans would have to take a backseat. “We’d got hailed out that summer,” he explains, “and [my dad] said, ‘Well, I can’t afford it.’ So he took me to a factory, a telephone and power cable factory. We both went in together.” Neill pauses to grapple with the emotional memory. Through tears, he shares how much his father meant to him: “I find this hard, as my dad isn’t here.” After taking a brief moment, he continues: “But anyway, walked into the Canada Wire and Cable office. And Sam Murray, the manager, says, ‘You’re hired.’ At 18 years of age. My dad says, ‘Holy, I should apply too!’ But he didn’t.”

Neill found that working at Canada Wire and Cable wasn’t an easy transition into the world of hourly employment: “It’s shift work. Changing shifts every week. And starting on the lowest rung on the ladder and eventually working my way up, running different machines.” To make matters even more difficult for the young worker, just over a year after being hired on, Neill’s tenure with Canada W&C experienced an unexpected interruption:

It was in the wintertime, working a midnight shift on the weekend, and…one of the senior fellows there, he come running over to me: “Fire! Fire!” This factory was in two or three old aeroplane hangars that they used for training pilots during World War II. And one of these factories caught on fire, so. And it destroyed the building. It was big old timbers and stuff. That was about 1970, I guess. And anyway, we were laid off for seven, eight months.

Until the factory could be rebuilt, Neill struggled to find another source of steady income. Since the fire had occurred during the winter, there was not much farm work available for Neil to fall back on. He found temporary employment helping a neighbour build corrals for cattle, but had little luck finding anything else. Disappointed, Neill collected unemployment until the W&C factory was once again in working order.

Once back at Canada W&C, Neill found that he was less willing to put up with the strain and stress of shift work, especially after the birth of his first child, a son: “That was kind of like a decision to leave, cause he was a very colicky baby, and trying to sleep and not having proper rest to go and work midnight shift…” In 1974, he quit his job at Canada W&C to become a house to house milkman. Though the pace of work was different from his job at the factory, driving a milk truck provided its own challenges for balancing work and family time, which had become even more important after the birth of his daughter in 1977:

Starting his career as a milkman in the mid seventies also meant that Neill had entered the industry during a period of restructuring:

They were franchising out each milk route. You had to buy it, and I had heard stories about other guys doing it and they lost their shirts, so I didn’t even attempt to do any of that. And that's how I ended up being a truck driver. Because the original truck driver, he needed to– we needed a spare driver. And as a farming background, they sort of pushed me into it, I guess you might say.

Even with its hectic schedule and increasing precarity, Neill says that being a milkman “was a good experience, too.” Working with dairy producers gave him the opportunity to learn the ins and outs of raising and handling dairy cows. “And I made friends, too.”

The next seachange in Neill’s work history came in 1984, when he returned home to take over the family farm from his parents:

They moved to town in about 1980. And the farm was getting very rundown with, you know, no upkeep and whatever. And it just killed me to go out there. And so my wife and I decided to go to the farm, and she wasn't very happy about it.

Though he found little joy in returning to the decaying property, Neill nevertheless felt a duty to try and continue the family legacy: “My mom always told me that someday this will be yours…I felt an obligation to take over the farm and be the third generation, I guess.”

The family’s first year back on the homestead proved inauspicious. That year, Neill’s father suffered a heart attack. And though he worked meticulously to cultivate the crop for that year, a dry growing season meant that Neill’s first harvest was a failure. “That year I’d kept track of rainfall,” he recalls, “We had four inches of rain. So we droughted out. My first claim for crop insurance.” This first year set the tone for the rest of Neill’s time as a farmer. For the next two decades, he and his family struggled to keep the farm profitable. His wife, Sherry, took a job, first at Agricultural Employment Canada and then at Southeast Regional College, to help supplement the family income, and Neill himself was forced to get a second job:

All the time on the farm, I drove a school bus. But there was no wage, there was just kind of– bought groceries, type of thing. And her income very much supported the farm. And it got hard, you know, her travelling back and forth.

After years of struggling to keep afloat, Neill eventually had to admit defeat. “2004, that was my last crop,” he says. Facing a mound of debt, Neill was forced to incorporate the farm. He knows that the choice was ultimately necessary, but more than a decade later, he is plagued by lingering doubts:

When I was farming with my dad, we just grew wheat...And then as farming practices changed, I grew peas and flax, I grew mustard, I never did grow canola. Barley, and oats. And in hindsight, maybe that was a mistake. Maybe if I had stayed to the regular ways of farming, as I was raised with…I’ve always had questions, but maybe– who knows?

Still, Neill says that the family’s life on the farm was not all doom and gloom: “We raised our kids there, and I think they had a pretty good life.”

Neill’s own quality of life was in question after incorporation of the farm. The sum he received from the government was not enough to support him and his wife long term, especially once lingering bills were paid. He went looking for work and applied around town, but nobody responded. “So I sent a resume to a company called Big Freight in Steinbach, Manitoba. I got a phone call right away, and [they said] ‘when can you be here?’” After a crash course in the finer points of trucking, Neill found himself sitting in the cab of a big rig, driving further afield than he ever had before: “I had never been east of Winnipeg. Next thing you know I’m east of Montreal and driving a truck!”

The freedom and novelty of the open road instantly clicked with Neill. When asked what it was that made trucking so enjoyable, Neill says that it was “the different sceneries. It made me feel like I was accomplishing something. I don’t know why, I just felt good about it.” Though trucking across Canada and the US meant periods away from his wife, it also gave him the opportunity to visit distant relations that he would otherwise not have had the time to connect with: “I’ve never seen, never met my relatives–or so many of them in such a short time–as [when] I was travelling. I have a brother and sister in Missouri. I got to see them lots.”

For Neill, the ability to spend time with family is no small thing. He and his family have experienced their fair share of grief over the years. Neill’s voice wavers slightly as he recounts the story of how his son-in-law was burned in an accident. He says that the long recovery period put a great deal of stress on the young family. When his daughter and son-in-law later divorced, Neill and his wife moved to Brooks in 2011 to help take care of their three grandchildren. A few years after this, his son-in-law died. Neill struggles to find the right words to describe the effect that this loss had on the family: “And so, another life changing experience.” Neill notes that as a result, his oldest granddaughter has become deeply involved in mental health advocacy, a vocation that she is intensely passionate about. Neill finds himself tearing up again, “Made me very proud.”

2007 was also the year that both Neill’s father and his wife’s Sherry’s mother also passed away. After processing the loss of so many family members in such a short time, Neill and his wife decided to remain in Brooks. Seeing an ad in the paper for the local United Farmers of Alberta (UFA), Neill applied for a job as a fuel truck driver and was hired on.

At the UFA, Neill and his coworkers are responsible for filling the trucks and then transporting fuel and gas to the company’s many clients, both in the city and at surrounding farms. He points out the importance of the indicators for whether there is diesel or gas in the line that runs from the truck’s reservoirs. “I could be going to another customer and forget [which one is in the line]. And you don’t want to put gas in the diesel. It’s happened!”

Though the job shares some similarities with his work as a big rig driver, Neill says the difference lies in the personal relationship that he shares with the people he sees every day during his rounds:

I love it. I love being here. Coming from an agricultural background, I deliver to farms…I can have some pretty decent conversations with farmers… I just relate to farmers. Lots of farmers, their kids played hockey with the grandsons, there's always a conversation about something. I recognise different farming practices and crops grown.

He is proud of the way his farming heritage connects him to his home city: “when I did get this job, that’s what I said to Sherry, is that I can relate to the community.”

Though the COVID pandemic did find its way to the small city of Brooks, Neill says that his work was not particularly affected by the virus:

My wife and I have both been vaccinated, and take precautions, and as far as working with COVID, being as I'm in the truck all the time, I don't really have to worry about it. I don't think. Dealing with customers on the farm, you know, it's pretty safe compared to being a cashier in Walmart, or a waitress,..or hospital. I can't imagine.

Unfortunately, he says that not everyone in the community felt the same way, and it caused difficulties at the United Farmers of Alberta (UFA) storefront: “We had you know, we locked the doors, and we had signs up, and people did not understand, and they’d rattle the door, you know.”

Now 70, Neill does not think he will be able to retire anytime soon. His thoughts once again return to the family farm:

While he doesn’t have much choice but to work to support himself and his wife, Neill also can’t quite imagine life without work: “Man, if I didn’t work, I don’t know where I’d be. It kind of scares me a bit, when I’m not going to be able to work. What am I going to do?”
