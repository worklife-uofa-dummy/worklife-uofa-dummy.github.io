Kendra’s Story

Kendra doesn’t spend much time reflecting on what it is that brought her back to ranching in southern Alberta after a stint as a banker in the nearby city of Brooks: “I’m kind of one of those people, I think your identity is whatever you want it to be? So I mean, there are so many, there’s so many things that could happen.” The ranching lifestyle is close to her heart, but she also has some new, external perspective on it.

Kendra grew up just a short horse ride away from the ranch that she now owns and operates with her husband, Willie. In many ways, Kendra has returned to her roots, building on much of the work that was built into her early life.

Right from the time we were little kids, I mean, we had chores, we had jobs, we did fencing. My parents didn't have quads at the time when I was at home, we had horses. So we started riding at a very early age, we helped check cows, we moved cattle. You know, in our teenage years, as soon as we were big enough to run a tractor we were doing baling and swathing. Dad always, we kept track of our hours and he would pay us I think like five bucks an hour or something like that… Just all the normal, the normal stuff. I guess it was paid work.

The rhythms of ranching life are familiar in some ways, and strange in others. Kendra and her husband, Willie, bought Crooked Tree Ranch from Willie’s former employers. Crooked Tree does more farming than Kendra’s family ranch, has more equipment, and is rapidly growing and diversifying. Ranch work is increasingly reliant on technology, and is gravitating away from the traditional form the industry took when Kendra was a young girl.

Although Kendra never expected to end up back at the ranch, it’s not an unwelcome move. She thinks back to how her teenage self might have perceived things:

You think you might, you think you might not. It's a tough one. Because when you're 18? I mean, you're like, ‘Yeah, I don't want to do this, I'm moving to the city.’ But then you're there for long enough and you're like, jeez, but you get used to it.

Kendra left home at 18 to attend the University of Lethbridge, loosely planning on taking up a career in accounting. She realized, quite quickly, that this wasn’t her path. “I liked bookkeeping, but I hated accounting. The thing I hated about accounting was how grey of an area, there’s never a right answer to any rule. And that just drove me up the wall.” Kendra stuck with it though, and even opted to add Finance as a major so that she wouldn’t feel stuck to one path. During university she also began working with a financial planning company, and realized that she had a gift for personal finance. After graduating, Kendra moved on to work at Scotiabank in the nearby city of Lethbridge, handling housing loans.

As Kendra settled into the city and a new career, she found love for what was, at first, a hobby and then turned into a lucrative side business as the Alberta oil economy took off in the first decade of the 2000s: buying, renovating, and renting out houses.

I started with Scotiabank, and then within a couple years is when the boom hit. So that kind of started my second career because I was working at the bank doing loans. So I understood how loans and rental properties work. I took the equity of my original house, and then I started buying houses. So what I would do is like, my little house that I bought was structurally sound, because I didn't know enough about structure and electrical and playing around that way. But I could do cosmetic, so I bought the house, which was structurally sound, and just cosmetically needed work. So then I worked on that one until it was all nice and pretty. And then I rented it out. And then I bought another one that was a dump. I moved in there. And then I fixed that up. And then that's what I’d do every six months to a year.

Kendra began making connections and had soon built up a network of people who would work on her houses. She enjoyed the work, but redoing the houses while working for the bank was exhausting, and Kendra felt a bit overwhelmed interviewing prospective tenants, reworking cosmetic issues, and having to move once or twice a year. She is disappointed that she was never truly able to enjoy the result of her labour; “I never got to live in one of my finished houses ever. I just kept going into a new dump.”

While working in Lethbridge, Kendra would make frequent trips back to Jenner to visit her parents’ ranch. “When I lived away, I always did come back to visit because I just needed a break from everything. Like this is my solitude, right, and I, I couldn’t stay away for like two three months.”

It was during these visits that she met Willie . They married shortly after their engagement, and Kendra moved into the double wide ranch house. Kendra’s life and routine quickly changed in the next couple of years as she became busy with full-time ranching and raising two young children. Kendra kept up her rental business for a period of time, but even with the support of a property manager, keeping that business running while working the ranch and raising young children was a struggle. “I ended up, it was too hard going with the kids and young kids, we were trying to go back and forth and like somebody moved out and you’d have to fix this and you have to update this. It was just way too much.”

About 2015, as Kendra began planning to sell her properties, her husband Willie was offered the opportunity to buy Crooked Tree Ranch, where he had worked for many years. Kendra’s rental properties and Willie’s land equity provided them with enough capital to begin transitioning from employees to owners. Shifting away from managing properties allowed Kendra to play a more active role in the ranch and bring more of her skill set to this new joint venture with her husband.

Ranching was a significant lifestyle change for Kendra, and in many ways re-shaped her work life to be an extension of things she loves. Her previous work had felt more like a way to meet her material needs, with hobbies squeezed in separately around her work schedule.

I've got a house paid for, I've got this, I need income and I, you know, you can't, can't live without income. So I have this job that, you know, I enjoy, but I have these other things I like doing. Whereas with farming and ranching, everything is so tied in together, I guess. The majority of what you enjoy doing and your hobbies are also tied to the ranch at the same time.

Still, Kendra feels a need to separate some of her personal pursuits from the business of ranching. Riding horses is a prime example. When she was a girl, she learned to navigate ranch work on horseback but it didn’t feel like work; today, as an adult, she reserves the horses for a “getaway” distinct from the temporal demands of the ranch.

I use the horses and the girls like to ride but we're not using them for ranch work because then it becomes a job. And if that's your thing that you like doing as your hobby or your pleasure, you hate to bring it over into the work because all of a sudden, it's not your little getaway. That's my little freedom or time away, like when I was a kid. I go out and I look at things that I enjoy, the scenery, and there's no, there's no work attached to it and there's no time frame attached to it. I can just enjoy.

These distinct moments apart from work are important, given the 24/7 demands of juggling the intertwined activities of family and ranching. As she puts it, “your house is your work,” and when you work with your spouse, “you never have a separate life.”

Kendra attributes divorce in the ranching community to the challenge of balancing a romantic relationship alongside a working one, but also notes the isolation and tough-it-out mentality. The COVID pandemic amplified these experiences.

[Mental health] has always been shunned in farming and ranching. But [with COVID] it's even worse, people have actually gone backwards instead of forward is my personal opinion. You're working these 24 hours a day, and you've got this business... So now you're just isolating all these people at their house. And then you wonder why half of them are alcoholics, or have issues, or, you know, it's changed, probably for the worse.

Isolation at home was a challenge for Kendra’s family but it also offered some respite from the demands of travel and community involvement: long bus rides to school for her children, long drives for weekly extra-curricular activities, and the expectations of regular gatherings and events in the community. The pandemic helped Kendra recognize the stress of these activities: “We were super busy… But there was so much more for me than Willie. Like, for me, there was so much outside stuff that was happening, that I didn’t realize what was happening. Yeah, of course you get stressed out, you’ve got like four things on the calendar there, that’s taken up half your day. When those went away, it was a nice thing. It was good.” Kendra wants to return to some of these outside community activities, but also realizes that this will need to be balanced against the load she’s been carrying.

The pandemic also introduced financial and operational challenges to their lives. Kendra’s family has been feeling the effects of inflation, fewer government supports, and smaller margins within the farming and ranching industries. Like in many other industries, a decline in the supply chain affected their ability to order parts and maintain their operations.

Working on the ranch is always demanding, but thankfully, the former owner, Stan, is still enjoying the work and able to help. Kendra and Willie briefly considered taking on a hired man, but Kendra struggles to justify the expense. After budgeting and weighing the increased cost and output, she suspects they would just break even. This is complicated by their diversification efforts, especially with cash crops.

We've almost created a monster here, because we have so many different facets. It’s working now–well, it's kind of working. We wouldn't even want to attempt to add another facet on unless we got a hired man, or we made some major changes. Like for Willie, he's putting in way longer hours than he should. He's exhausted. Stan puts in what he can but he can only do so much. Same thing: I can only do so much with the kids.

Kendra describes her work and her daily routine on the ranch in seasons. In the winter, Kendra’s main job is caring for the sick calves, something Willie has the capacity to do but often lacks time. Willie would feed the sick calves while Kendra doctored them. The needs of the calves vary depending on their numbers and the weather conditions outside. In the winter, things slow down a bit around the ranch, but Kendra still finds herself quite busy between organizing the kids for school and checking calves with Stan.

Usually Stan and I, like after I get the kids on the bus, I go down and then between the two of us we've kind of split up the work. If the water freezes, check them. He usually goes to the far end, and I start at this end and you got to walk through the pens if there's any sick calves, pull them out, bring them in. I usually I do all the treating. Stan’ll pull some out for me, but I'll take them down and treat them and put them back in their pen, which can take all morning, or could take a half hour. Depends on how cold it is.

During the spring calving season, storms or inclement weather mean bringing the calves indoors, which Kendra also takes care of. She notes that she’s the one feeding the bottle calves “because nobody likes doing that.” When branding season comes around, she orders the vaccines, and if cattle are being moved, she helps with that, too, “plus whatever with the kids.”

As Kendra’s children grow, she’s involving herself more in their activities and extracurriculars. She’s also bringing some skills from her work experience as a financial manager and home renovator to support the ranch and her growing family. She draws on her finance background to provide Willie with details of how new ventures, such as purchasing new equipment or incurring other additional expenses, would affect their bottom line. These skills were front and center when she and Willie made arrangements with Stan and Robin to officially assume ownership of Crooked Tree Ranch.

When she can, Kendra spends time DIYing and renovating their double-wide home. She recalls how she set to work when her two daughters declared they wanted separate bedrooms. Kendra refinished and painted an entire room before her daughters returned from school at 4:00 pm. She brought in new furniture, dismantled and re-assembled baseboards, and found new homes and storage solutions for furniture that was no longer in use. “I get things cleaned up, and then I just wreck it all and create chaos again and have to start over,” she laughs.

Kendra feels she is well-suited to her job of organizing amidst heavy and changing demands both at home and on the ranch, describing herself as someone who struggles to sit still and who moves through life calmly and with flexibility. She has learned to “roll with the punches,” such as new regulations around tagging and branding cattle. After all, it was this flexibility that brought her back to working on the ranch.

As their girls get older, Kendra sometimes questions these taken-for-granted assumptions. “You kind of get to a point–which we are getting to–of like, how much do you really want to do, and not enjoy anything, especially as the girls are getting– They’re growing up, and we’re missing a lot of it.”

Kendra loves the ranching life, but also can imagine the different paths her life might have taken. She thinks back to when she was younger, contemplating being a bartender or doing an exchange program in Europe. “So I mean, there are so many, there’s so many things that could happen.”
