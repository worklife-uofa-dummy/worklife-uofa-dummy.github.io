Dwayne’s Story

“That has a screw pump on it. Not a pumpjack,” Dwayne says, pointing out the truck window as dust swirls around the oil well. He explains:

It's got the motor on there…and it drives a big pulley attached to that rod that’s coming up. And it just rotates it, it goes all the way down about 900 metres. The pump at the bottom and it just rotates. You know how a grain auger works? Same idea. . . So unlike a pump jack, which goes up and down, it has basically a bicycle tire pump at the bottom. It’s a different principle.

Having tended to this kind of machinery for most of his working life (and this particular machine for over a decade), Dwayne knows it inside and out. A contract oil operator, he spends most of his days traversing the backroads of rural Alberta in his truck, inspecting the various pumps and batteries owned by his clients.

When asked about how smaller operations like these fit into the oil industry as a whole, Dwayne explains that “Alberta is full of these little projects. For big companies to operate them doesn’t make a lot of sense because they don’t have a lot of importance to the company as a single unit.” But the owners of these small projects appreciate Dwayne’s service and experience:

The most satisfying part is talking with my supervisors, being able to provide input and having them consider it. That's huge. And a lot of places you can't do that. You don't get that. But I've been very fortunate.

At each stop on his rounds, Dwayne dismounts from his truck, moving to carefully inspect the complex mechanisms that are responsible for pumping up the fuel and gas that comprises the backbone of the province’s economy. Any sign of an issue, and Dwayne has to make a phone call. In the modern oil industry, the old adage that “time is money” holds especially true. He points out that a pump that is damaged or underproducing quickly loses its value.

Though Dwayne has worked steadily and successfully as a contract operator for nearly a decade now, his history in the industry is marked with the ups and downs typical of the careers of many of the young men who sought their fortunes during the 1970s Alberta oil boom. He has lived through his fair share of layoffs, injury, and job hopping. Along the way, he has cultivated the knowledge, skills, and work ethic needed to succeed in an industry where some only manage a few good years of employment: “[I]t’s been quite a ride, believe me.”

Dwayne’s first experience of work came courtesy of his parents’ farm. Located just north of Edmonton, his family’s modest homestead had little in common with today’s large corporate agricultural operations: “It was just a small little dairy farm. About 30, 40 cows…Back then that was fairly normal.” Beginning at the age of 12, Dwayne began to help his father with the many day-to-day tasks that were required. Dwayne credits these early years of work with his father as the source of his rise and grind mindset: “Early up and get out…that was my dad. My dad worked hard. His dad worked hard.” All told, Dwayne counts himself lucky to have had a childhood that he knows many others didn’t:

All my needs were covered. I still feel I was one of the luckiest people alive to be born and raised where I was, just fantastic. Had a great family. Very loving parents. Very, very fortunate. Yeah. I thought that's the way it was for everybody for the longest time. Then I realized: no, that's not the case.

Even so, as young adulthood approached, Dwayne decided that he had no desire to follow in his father’s footsteps: “It’s the same situation fathers and sons have around the world. Very, very difficult to work for your dad.” Dwayne originally “wanted to pursue law,” but found himself drawn towards a different focus:

I left home I guess when I was 18, 19…I went to a school in Edmonton…My final year of highschool was M.E. LaZerte in Edmonton…It was a magnificent school. Way too good for kids! The technical wing of it was fantastic. It had aircraft maintenance. And they had several aircraft there to work on. And that was kind of an interest because I had gone to Air Cadets as a kid. And so I thought…I could do this but I was still way too young and too much into partying and whatnot to take a career seriously. So that kind of went by the wayside.

Dwayne did find some work as an aircraft mechanic at the Edmonton Flying Club after graduation from M.E. LaZerte, but ultimately the stakes of aircraft maintenance were too high for a young man still figuring things out. He gave up on aircraft maintenance as a career because “the responsibility at that young age” was too much for him to handle. “It hangs over your head,” he says, “you do something the slightest bit wrong, and there’s other people dead.”

Having discovered that being an aircraft mechanic wasn’t for him, Dwayne began the nomadic approach to employment that would characterize much of his work life. Moving to Vancouver, he found work manufacturing floats for Twin Otter seaplanes, followed by a short stint repairing train cars for CP Rail in Port Coquitlam.

Like many other young men of the time, Dwayne eventually found himself being pulled back to his home province by the economic promise of the 1970s oil boom. His brother-in-law had started an oil and gas testing company in Calgary, so Dwayne moved back to Alberta. It didn’t take long to fall in love with the freedom and monetary reward that working in the oil industry provided him:

It was so cool, you know, the oil industry, then. If you…put any effort into it, you would get rewarded. And that's just how they were, they would reward you financially, they would reward you with more responsibility. It was just interesting.

This good pay for hard work was Ideal for Dwayne. For him, so much of what makes work worthwhile is having one’s efforts recognized and appreciated: “To be able to get that, all that–and all it is is respect…So many jobs, you don’t get that….It makes a huge difference.” During this time, Dwayne also got married to his first wife.

As the late-80s rolled around, oil prices fell. Dwayne found himself among the workers who were laid off as the industry adopted cost saving measures. He also found himself ill-equipped. The work ethic developed during Dwayne’s early years on the family dairy farm hadn’t prepared him for the experience of being down and out:

Ultimately though, Dwayne was one of the lucky ones. His experience as a gas tester and willingness to endure less than ideal working conditions gave him a solid backup plan for the inevitable dips in the cycle of boom and bust:

I knew I could always fall back on gas well testing because I had experience with it [and it] wasn't something that everybody wanted to do. You know, you're driving out in the middle of nowhere, and you're hooking on to this well that could be a monster, and can be dangerous. And that's cold, you're living in a camper right there on site. You are responsible for keeping things running or you're gonna get cold. So it wasn't something that appealed to everybody. But it did to me, so…I knew I could always go back and do that, even up until, say, 10 years ago, if that ever became an issue.

While his particular skills helped Dwayne secure new work, it also meant relocating on a not infrequent basis. Taking jobs as they came, Dwayne spent the next couple decades migrating around the province. From 1985-1990 Dwayne worked as a power engineer at a facility not far from Bonnyville.

Looking to make a go at domesticity, Dwayne and his wife “bought a quarter section at Iron River,” which they shared with their two children. After his wife enrolled at the University of Alberta in Edmonton, Dwayne and his family moved to Bon Accord, a small town about 20 minutes north of the provincial capital, to stay close to his family . But Dwayne would not settle there for long. His first marriage ended in 1991, and Dwayne once again found himself uprooted:

I went back to gas well testing, which is where I had started out…And then switched over to Canadian Forces Base (CFB) Edmonton. Went from there to an operating job at Maidstone, Saskatchewan, Lloydminster, Kitscoty…I was on my own there for a couple years.

“Always [trying] to move up on to something better,” Dwayne continued to move around quite a bit until he met his second wife during his time in Lloydminster. They settled in Medicine Hat for six years, from 1997-2003. And then in 2007, after nearly two decades spent playing a game of musical chairs in the fossil fuel job market, Dwayne and his wife landed in southern Alberta, near Jenner. They’ve remained there ever since, making it, in Dwayne’s words: “Longest place I’ve been, other than where I was born.”

Being part of the Alberta oil industry since the 1970s has given Dwayne a front row seat to shifts in the discourses and practices of the energy industry. He notes the push for greener sources of energy and environmental regulation. Driving past a wind turbine, Dwayne remarks: “What a horrendous waste of money.” True to his affinity for the mechanical, he frames his objections on technical grounds:

[A]s fascinated as I am with the technology, which I am, I’d love to see exactly how a windmill works, the turbine and the gear reduction and all of that. And the power produced. That's amazing. But it's not always there when you need it. So until they can get that [electricity] storage thing sorted out…Without that, what's the point? And it's very, very expensive. It could be 50 years or 100 years til they sort that out.

But in Dwayne’s opinion, the biggest change to the energy sector has been the exponential encroachment of government regulation:

I'm not a great one for doing exactly as the government instructs. I go back to when I first started in the oil fields. Hardhats were okay, maybe, and coveralls, no. That was dependent on if you didn’t want to get your street clothes dirty. Then I believe there was a fire at a plant near Empress, would have been back in the 80s and there was a dozen guys that were terribly horribly burned as a result of that fire…as a result of that, in Alberta, immediately was those regulations for having to wear fireproof coveralls. Try wearing those on the plus 35 degrees. In the summertime, short sleeves weren't an option.

This is not to say that Dwayne disagrees with every safety measure that has been introduced to the industry, merely those measures that he thinks are “overkill” or unnecessary: “You know, I’m not against putting safety where it’s supposed to be, but something worthwhile.”

Oilfield safety is not an abstract issue for Dwayne. He recalls one particular accident from his early career that nearly cost him his life:

Injured and struggling against the elements, Dwayne’s life was saved by a water truck driver that managed to spot him and haul him into the warmth of his vehicle. The accident has left Dwayne with a limp, an injury which has never fully faded. Though he has had to live with it for over forty years, Dwayne says that his leg’s decreased mobility has not caused him undue trouble in his day-to-day work: “I’m very fortunate with my injury that it really has not affected me that much. I’m slow going up and down stairs. And I don’t run…” He follows this last observation with a wry chuckle.

Despite the fact that Dwayne’s brush with death came when he was working alone, he sees the solitary independence that oil well operation provides him as one of the biggest perks of his work:

I have a lot in common with the farmers here, because we're both equally independent that way. Specifically, because I'm working largely here on my own. I'm a long ways away from all the other projects…[A]s long as you do your job and do it reasonably well, there's always somebody else they’re looking at…So yeah, you can live quite independently.

The solitary nature of Dwayne’s job provided an unexpected fringe benefit during the COVID pandemic. Mostly operating alone, driving from well to well across the wide open landscape of rural Alberta for what was deemed an “essential industry,” Dwayne’s work remained largely unaffected by the deadly virus. The only major disruption that Dwayne experienced during the pandemic years was not being able to travel to Calgary to see his sister: “That’s a tough one. But really, that would be the extent of it.”

Family connection and loss are a significant part of Dwayne’s experience. In 2010, his daughter died: “That was a very tough one…That was, drugs. That was what did that.” Like many parents who have suffered the loss of a child, Dwayne has struggled to come to terms with his daughter’s absence: “It doesn’t always get much better…” Though the demands of his work schedule sometimes keep him away from family, Dwayne makes an effort to see his grandchildren on a regular basis: “She had two children…So I keep a keen eye on them.”

Dwayne has also found kinship with some of his long-time working relationships in the oil field. In considering one operation that he has tended for over two decades, Dwayne expresses a deep affection for the ranch where the oil wells are located and its owners: “They’ve been so good to us, treat us like family here…So I was here when we built it, you know, so in 1997…I’ve been a part of its history right from the beginning.” At the end of the day, he finds it deeply satisfying to do something that others find valuable and to have his efforts recognized: “You have to be able to contribute…And to have it appreciated. Those two things, to be able to contribute and have it appreciated. Yeah. Those are massive.”

Despite creeping towards his seventh decade of life, Dwayne has no hard plans to quit working: “That would drive me nuts. I will work as long as I possibly can.” At the same time, he is realistic about his ability to continue to meet the demands of his job as he ages:

I would imagine, in five years, I will have a difficult time, I can’t see myself working beyond that. Because just the physical requirements of the job would be what's difficult. And mentally…I don't want to get to the point where I'm not mentally astute enough to be able to continue on – become a liability, basically.

But for Dwayne, leaving the workforce would not mean resting on one’s laurels. Retirement would give him a chance to finally pursue the hobbies that he has struggled to find time for while working:

I love woodworking, I love old stereo equipment. I can tell you recently, I've started doing something that I have wanted to do for many many years, but haven't had the time. And that's building wooden ship models.

Ultimately, whether it’s oil well maintenance, woodworking, or model shipbuilding, the specifics of the work are less important than the act of working itself. A perpetually restless soul, Dwayne finds himself ill suited to sitting still: “Whether I’m getting paid or not, I’m still [going to] get up and get going and do something.”
