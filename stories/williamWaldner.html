William’s Story

Willie minces no words when describing his feeling of liberation as a young man leaving home: “I hate to put it so bluntly, but it’s almost like coming out of jail.” At 58 years old, Willie is the proud owner of a family cattle ranch in southern Alberta, but as a child, Willie’s life looked very different from what it has now become.

Willie spent his adolescence as a member of a Hutterite community, an Anabaptist religious sect that eschews many parts of modern technology and culture. Willie says that being raised in this self-sufficient farming community instilled him with the work ethic that has carried him through his adult life. Even so, it was never going to be a good fit. “Growing up in a Hutterite colony, you are limited as to what you can do in life,” he explains, “and I just wanted more.” His desire for “more” is evident not just in Willie’s decision to leave the colony but throughout his career in agriculture. From his childhood in the colony, up through his years as a ranch hand, and even into his current position as the owner of his own ranch, Willie has approached his labour with a kind of realistic pragmatism. For him, working means earning and independence, both for himself and for those he holds dear: “There’s the things in life that I want. I need money to get those things. I have to work.”

One of 11 children, Willie was raised by his parents in Springside Colony, a Hutterite community located just north of Duchess, Alberta. From birth to early adulthood, his upbringing was marked by strict religious observance and the seasonal rhythms of communal agriculture. Educated in both English and Hutterite German, the boys of the colony would spend eight months out of the year in a cyclical routine of study and prayer. Willie recounts his daily schedule: “The day started out with German school, English school for most of the day, and then a shorter German school, supper, and then a short church service.” During this time of the year, Willie’s work outside of school was kept relatively light: “They didn’t expect you to do too much other than on the weekend. You had a little bit [of chores], but we just loved doing it.”

Though German school would continue throughout the entire year, the start of the growing season would mark a transition point. English school would be let out, with Willie and the other boys instead tasked with assisting the older Hutterites with the many chores required to maintain the colony and ensure a successful harvest. This represented a different kind of education. The boys would be cycled through various roles from year to year, trying their hand at everything:

Two or three of us would… help the chicken boss. Two or three of them you'd go to the dairy farm. Three or four of them go to the hog operation. Next one, on, and on, and on... They just had it divvied out. And you would change, like one year you would do this, and the following year you would do that, the next year that, and so on.

Learning the ins and outs of farming, animal husbandry, carpentry, construction, mechanics, and more kept Willie and the other boys busy throughout the Summer and Autumn. Between the obligations of school, church, and work, “there was always something that you did,” Willie says. “You had very, very little time for free play time, so to speak.” Due to a philosophy of communal ownership, Willie and the other Hutterite boys were expected to undertake this work for the collective good of the community. Willie credits this regimented structure of Hutterite life as the source of his drive to work:

I had a responsibility at a very early age. [We] already had certain chores…They expected it of you and you were proud. You did not want to be a weak link. You just did it. That's how I always thought, and everybody around me just did it….You didn't know any better. Right or wrong. You just did it. And I've done it my whole life.

Though Willie says his parents did a “heck of a job” raising him, the strictness and rigidity of Anabaptist philosophy and culture grated on him. As he approached adulthood, the friction between him and the community steadily grew. While he holds no resentment against the community in which he was raised, Willie explains that: “The one thing I have against it, was nobody ever told me what I was learning. They never took time to explain to me as to why.”

Eventually, Willie decided that living the rest of his life in the colony was not the future he wanted. In 1984, a moment of serendipity arose when his best friend returned to the Hutterite community after spending a year and a half away. Knowing that his friend had more knowledge of life outside the colony than he did, Willie approached him for help: “I just told him, ‘Hey, if you [could] just ask around, and [let me know] if you find anybody in that area that is looking for help, or where I could possibly go to work.’”

Willie’s friend was quick to return with an opportunity. A neighbour near his work, Stan, would be willing to take him on as a ranch hand. “Well that was all it took,” Willie says, matter-of-factly:

But you are not allowed to tell anybody. It's like, I hope to God my kids never do to me what I did to my parents, but I had no choice. If you want to leave the colony, you can't tell anybody. You can’t tell your sister, you can’t tell your brother in fear that they would tell mom and dad, or anybody else, and then they would watch you 24 hours a day. Then they would not permit it. They don't want you to leave, of course. When he said he found somebody, it was between him and I. And we had it pre-arranged. He came out one evening and I gathered up the few things I had, without telling anybody and I came out here.

But after succeeding in his goal of leaving the colony, Willie quickly found that adjusting to his new surroundings presented its own struggles. Leaving behind everything he knew to live and work among strangers required Willie to reorient himself in an environment that was wildly different from what he had previously experienced. He describes the culture shock that he experienced in his first months outside the colony:

You're just a young kid, you're lonely, you don't have anything. And it's so different. The food is different. It is literally like parachuting out of a plane jumping somewhere. And here you go. Start up and do what you got to. See what you're made out of. I don't wish it on anybody.

Willie’s alienation was further intensified by his outsider status. His bilingual schooling in childhood had left him with a noticeable accent, one that allowed him to be identified by others as a former member of the Hutterite community As a result, Willie’s neighbours and peers would sometimes make fun of him. They would refer to him as “The Hutterite,” an epithet that made him feel ostracised. “You don’t want to be singled out,” Willie says. “You want to fit in, you want to be part of that community, and so on. And when they refer to you as [The Hutterite], that just– It’s just like a blow.”

His feelings of isolation were also heightened by the comparatively solitary labour of work on a small ranch. With many duties of his own, Stan would often spend hours away from the property, trusting Willie with a list of tasks to accomplish. “That was a little frightening for me,” Willie admits, “because I grew up with people around me all the time. I had hundreds of people all around me I was working with.” He recalls one memorable incident involving a visit from a neighbour:

He rode across the river with a horse. His combine had broke down and he was looking for a combine part…But I just happened to be in the yard here someplace and I see a horse coming from over there. I ran and hid. I ran and hid because I didn't know. I was scared…He was actually a very nice guy.

Over time, Willie began to feel more comfortable in his new home. While still acclimating, Willie found himself “hooked” on the freedom that came from earning a living outside the colony. In contrast to his work in the Hutterite community, his free time and the fruits of his labour were his to do with as he pleased: “You get paid, you got your own money. I can go to town, I can buy a case of beer, I can buy a pack of cigarettes. I can, you know, if I see a girl, I can go talk to a girl.”

Willie’s transition was also made easier by his new employers, Stan and Robin. The couple not only provided Willie with employment, but also food, shelter, and mentorship. In time, they came to treat Willie as a member of the family. Willie speaks fondly of the couple’s kindness. “They grew up almost as my parents,” he says warmly. “They treated me good and I treated them good. And so on. I think the world of them. They gave me a hell of an opportunity in life.”

Willie, in turn, studiously applied the work ethic that he had developed during his adolescence to his duties on the couple’s ranch: “[Stan] would ask me to go do something. And within a few hours I was done, where he would expect you to take a whole day to do it. And I’m like, ‘Okay, now what’s next?’” Over the years, his jack-of-all trades proficiency has earned him an alternative nickname among his neighbours: “I know people in the community called me ‘the fixer.’ I’ve heard people call me ‘the fixer’ and I’m like, I don’t think I’m the fixer. I just have all the things down.”

Willie would also go on to start his own family. “That was the final piece to the puzzle,” he says. Willie and his wife, Kendra, have been married for 11 years, though they had been dating for some time before finally deciding to tie the knot. The couple have also had two daughters together. Willie says that it has not been overly difficult balancing his time between raising his daughters and his ranch work, but admits that as they grow older it is getting “a little more complex.” Extracurricular activities like 4H, dancing, and gymnastics represent significant commutes from the family’s rural home, making it necessary to pick and choose: “I told them we have to make sacrifices in life, doing what we’re doing [as ranchers]. And I really believe that they both know it.”

Building a family has also gradually shifted Willie’s motivations for working. Earning a wage is increasingly about supporting his wife and daughters: “That’s my focus in life now. I want to do what’s best for them…And that’s what I work for. I want to do the best I can for them. And I’m doing it every day.”

Willie started dating Kendra in 2008 and they got married in 2010. His relationship with Kendra would also give Willie the opportunity to purchase Stan’s ranch, something that he says would have never been possible through his own means. Willie tears up as he recounts the story:

She owned four houses in Lethbridge, she had her own house and then she had rental properties in Lethbridge, and she sold every one of those things within the first year when we were dating – to pool that money. And that's the money that we eventually used…I will forever be grateful for her for doing that.

Coming from a banking and finance background, Kendra also remains in charge of the financial side of the couple’s division of labour. “I leave it up to her,” Willie says, “and I trust her with every ounce of me in that regard. And she does very well.”

Owning his own land has also given Willie a sense of greater purpose. When asked how he would describe what he does, he says: “I’m proud to be a rancher. But I’m also proud to be a farmer. If you combine the two. I am a producer.” Through his work raising cows and cultivating cash crops, Willie has managed to carve out a life where he feels that he can be independent while also contributing to society:

Everything that you do has more meaning to it. It has a purpose and you set goals. I set more goals, way more goals, now than before I owned [the ranch]…Because it goes back to pride, you know? I want my neighbours to think, “Oh, hey, he's doing a pretty good job!”

Thinking towards the future, Willie hopes to maintain the ranch’s independence. With more and more farms being conglomerated under corporate ownership, he worries that some of that freedom might be lost. “We’re getting squeezed out,” he says. He believes that small ranches and farms like his are able to embrace approaches to agriculture beyond the mere profits sought by agricultural companies:

If I want to turn this place into, like, organic farming, where I don't apply herbicides or chemicals or things to the crops that I produce, I have that choice. The same thing with cattle, if I want to raise hormone free cattle, and so on. [If I’m squeezed out] all those things will be gone.

For the time being, Willie says that he will work as long as he can. “I most definitely don’t want to be referred to as a bum that sits around and collects welfare,” he says. “I would, you know, wake up from my grave if someone called me that. Retirement is not in [me].”

Still, Willie finds the time to engage in hobbies. A gun owner, he finds pleasure in making his own ammunition, experimenting with cartridge sizes and powder types that he then uses while hunting. Though he sometimes sells the pelts of his trophies for extra income, he says that he mostly hunts for the sake of personal satisfaction: “I just enjoy doing it. It’s a sport and I skin it and then I prepare the hide and I take it to the auction. A few times I’ve won an award for doing it.”

Looking back on his life, from his childhood in Springside Colony, through to the kindness of strangers, the construction of new family, and finally obtaining a piece of land to call his own, Willie says he has derived one particular takeaway:

It’s tough in life, and you need breaks in life. And the other thing I will say is that–I have told a lot of people that–”Dream big,” absolutely. You go to graduations, and everybody says dream big . . . and stay true to your dreams. And I repeat that absolutely. 100%. If I can do it, anybody can do it.
