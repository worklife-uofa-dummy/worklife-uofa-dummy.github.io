One day in 1985, Dave received a letter that would change his life: an offer to work full-time in the Whitby Fire Department. He thinks back to “how lucky he was,” especially in comparison to the precarious work and exorbitant housing costs faced by his children’s generation.

I was 24 years old. I was younger than my three kids are now and I had my career set for the next thirty years. And I already owned my first house. So, you compare that to where kids are nowadays, it’s quite a difference. I mean, I knew how fortunate I was to get on at the Fire Department. And I was determined to do well there. So, that was the start of my career.

Dave stayed for thirty years, moving up through the ranks to Platoon Chief. Now retired, he has had time to reflect on the profound effects of this chosen career on his life. He has suffered compounded trauma and loss, the story of his career structured by a series of difficult calls and ever-more challenging responsibilities. At the same time, Dave has enjoyed the deep camaraderie among firefighters and the satisfaction of helping people in crisis situations. “Despite all the ups and downs, I wouldn’t change it for anything, you know. I’d go back and do another 30 years. It’s just, it’s special—there’s a special bond there.” This makes the adjustment to retirement especially hard.

Dave grew up in Whitby, where his father worked at Bell Telephone and his mother cared for the family at home before working at Sears for a while. Dave recalls his father coming home from work and talking about the guys who didn’t do a good job and muses that this probably shaped his own management style. “I remember him often saying ‘You know, if you treat a man like a man, then he’ll act like a man.’” As a teenager, Dave worked in the summers pumping gas and helping at a Go-Cart track. Once he started college, he worked summers and weekends for Bell, installing phone jacks and later doing residential repair. He points out that “a lot of kids back then ended up in the same field for summer jobs as your parents worked.” He is amazed, looking back, that at 18 years of age he was staying in hotels with an expense account for meals. While some other employees didn’t like the travel, Dave thought it was great: “I was getting paid to drive!”

It was serendipity that steered Dave toward firefighting. Having finished his degree in marketing in 1983, Dave worked for a company that sold business machines, but found he was quite unhappy—in part because the economic recession meant few sales. He knew he needed to make a change.

So I went down to the manpower agency one day, and I just happened to run into a friend from the neighbourhood and he said ‘what do you do?’ And I said, ‘Well, I'm looking for a job. I don't like the one I got.’ And he said, ‘Well, why don't you put your name in at the fire department?’ He worked there at the time. And I said, ‘I've never really thought about that.’ And he said, ‘Well come on, we we’re only about two blocks from the main station. Let's go do it right now. So, we walked down, and I put my name in.

Dave started firefighter training within a couple of weeks and was immediately hooked. “It was exciting, something that I knew wouldn’t be the same every day. And something where I felt like I was helping people.” While in training Dave worked a depressing factory shift job but had promised himself that if he didn’t get hired on at the fire department within two years, he would find something else. As it happened, it was almost two years to the day that he got that letter offering full-time employment at the fire station. Dave chokes up, remembering that feeling. He was “set for life,” and what’s more, he and his wife, Linda, could stay in his hometown of Whitby.

Dave started where most firefighters started back then, in dispatch, working the 25 phone lines. With all the communication over the radio going through the dispatcher, “it gave you a very good idea of how the job worked.” From there, employees moved up by seniority, and within two years Dave was working as a probationary firefighter. Taking the yearly exams, he moved up quickly to first class firefighter, and ten years later he successfully wrote the captain’s exam.

In those early years, Dave was known as “the white cloud” because nothing bad seemed to happen around him. The major emergency calls would almost always come while he was off rotation, after which the crew would “love nothing better than to phone and tell you about the big fire you missed.” When he did get his first big call, shortly after becoming Acting Captain—where he was thus in charge of a truck and crew—everything went well, giving him some confidence. But this changed over time, and the stress mounted. Even emergency incidents that turned out okay contributed, given the combination of adrenaline and responsibility. As Dave tells it, the deepening stress unfolded in chapters, each one built around a traumatizing incident that he marks by the year he was on the job. There was a residential fire in a two-storey house in his eighteenth year, where his crew was first to arrive. During the first crucial minutes of size-up it appeared to be a second-floor fire. Two crew members advanced to the second floor when the first floor began burning out of control, trapping them above the fire and they were forced to call a mayday.

It was really touch and go…but they got them out. And that’s when the job really changed for me. Because I realized how close I came to losing two guys... once you've had those outcomes, and you feel that feeling of how close you came, then you know, you never want to feel that again... From then on, the job was different. I thought about it a lot more when I was off duty. Night shifts were always harder because that's usually when the worst fires come in.

Back then, in the early 2000s, he was on four-night shifts rather than the 24-hour shifts used now. Dave describes starting to get apprehensive two days before the start of a shift. “I’d just be thinking to myself, like, you know, are we going to get something big or aren’t we, and if we do, will I make the right decisions or won’t I.”

The next big incident was in Dave’s twenty-fourth year, right after he became Acting Platoon Chief (the first step to assuming Platoon Chief) and was thus in charge of all five fire stations in the area. At a call for a house fire, misinformation coincided with a flashover, a sudden and intense ball of flame. Four firefighters barely made it out.

I’m at the point where I’m taking over this job in a couple of months. And I’ve just had my second horrendous call with good luck or good management, and everybody has come out okay. But again, it amped up my apprehensiveness and uneasiness about the job.

Dave kept at it because he felt he was good at his job, and he derived satisfaction from rising to the challenge in a professional manner. It was also “the human factor” that kept him going: “Sometimes it can be as simple as holding somebody’s hand during a difficult time. It can be that simple.” Dave feels he has always been an empathetic person, and this both kept him on the job and made it emotionally and psychologically difficult.

Several years after the bad house fire call, the night shift alarm went off, and Dave found himself leaping out of bed to get dressed “like I knew it was going to be something special. And we got a call for a fire with people trapped on the second floor.” In this third traumatizing incident, three teenagers died. Two crew members received commendations, and Dave was extremely proud of how they handled things, but the incident haunted him.

The hiring of a new Chief with whom Dave and others had some profound disagreements only made things worse, and in 2013 Dave “just had a breakdown from everything.” He took an eight-month stress leave, becoming the first firefighter in his department to publicly acknowledge the toll of the work and take such a leave. Dave returned to the job, but another high stress call followed, and he soon decided to retire at 30 years of service—a milestone he had already planned toward.

I'd already made my mind up back, probably when the first incident took place at the 18-year mark, that if I could get 30 years on the job and nobody got hurt on my watch, I was leaving ... I could have gone to 35 years to increase my pension, but I made the decision if I get to 30 years I'm gone because if I stay to 31 or 32 and somebody gets hurt, I'll never forgive myself.

Dave’s departure was full of mixed emotions, given his deep connection to the work. “The day I drove out of there, it was like this huge weight had been lifted off my shoulders, [but] I still struggled a bit with the call and with the way everything ended.” He also struggled with leaving his crew members and the almost wordless bond they shared, not to mention the “extended family” of firefighters’ wives and families, who celebrated holidays and retirements together.

The work – family relationship has been complex. Dave tried to keep them separate. For most of his career and having grown up in that era of “boys don’t cry,” Dave kept his feelings to himself, “not even really sharing them with my co-workers or my wife, Linda.” But his job nonetheless affected his family and social life in several ways. His kids began to call him “Mr. Safety” because he was always reminding them of what could go wrong. As he says, you begin to see your own family in the people you help as a firefighter. So, although a lot of firefighters are second or third generation, Dave didn’t want his own boys to be firefighters. “I would just worry about them too much.” He would especially worry about “what kind of guys they’re working with, you know, are they sharp on the ball guys? Are they some cowboy that’s going to rush him when he shouldn’t?” Dave thinks his boys picked up on the stress early on and decided they had no interest.

At the same time, the extended days off in Dave’s shift schedule made it possible for him to spend time with family, enjoy social activities, and take on family care responsibilities.

It gave me the extra time off to spend with my kids. All the teachers at school knew my name, knew who I was, knew who my kids were ... And it gave me time off to do things I enjoyed—to play hockey, to play golf.

As his family configuration changed, so did his care activities. When he first started on the job, he was off during most of the week and could help his grandmother with groceries and errands—he became known as the “egg man” in her building as he went on fresh egg runs for a number of the senior residents. And then when his father died in 2001, Dave became the “go-to guy” for his mother. In retirement, Dave and Linda, moved in with Linda’s aging parents to help care for them and their property. After a brief battle with cancer, Linda passed away in 2020. Dave continued to live with his in-laws.

Dave saw a lot of change in his time in fire services, a number of which have implications for life beyond the job. The switch a number of years ago to a 24-hour shift schedule at the firehall, for example, means that “your wife, especially if she’s got young kids, is going through 24 hours on her own at home, or almost 48. [Because] you’re just wiped out after a 24-hour shift and the next 24 hours is a write-off . . . you’re not in a good mood, you’re tired.” This scheduling change was made, in part, to accommodate fewer long commutes for the Whitby firefighters residing in the Greater Toronto Area. Whitby’s proximity to Canada’s largest city has posed challenges for many years. Whitby moved to full-time positions several decades ago when they realized they were losing their newly trained part-time firefighters to the full-time jobs in the Toronto system. Dave points out that the fire service is not regionalized; this would allow for easier movement of equipment across towns, but would also mean crew members could be moved around, losing consistency and connection to place.

The most profound change Dave has seen, however, is in training and safety. Unlike when he was coming up through the system, firefighters now receive constant training and upgrading. In many ways the job is safer. Thanks in part to Dave, there is much more attention to stress and trauma. And physical safety has been improved through more protective gear and better equipment. The trucks have closed cabs “so that you aren’t riding out in the freezing cold,” and there are now thermal imaging cameras. Still, this comes with trade-offs. The new gear “provided you so much protection that you could get far deeper into the building before you realized how hot it was.” And the cameras could in some instances give you a false sense of security. And in the end, Dave says, training and equipment are not going to make you a good firefighter: “When it comes to a fire, man, you want the guy who knows what to do and he’s going to do it. There’s an innate natural ability to stay calm and to be able to focus on what needs to be done.”

Dave retired before COVID hit, but the stresses the firefighters face remind him of when the SARS pandemic hit. There was uneven adoption of protective practices, the worry about bringing SARS home to family members, and losing the connection to community through open house days at the station or bringing a truck and crew to community events.

More palpable for Dave was the effect of COVID on his own grown children, one of whom works in a large restaurant company where “of course, everything went for shit” and she was laid off. One of his sons also got laid off but eventually was re-hired. It was hard for Dave to witness this, especially given the general instability for his children’s generation. “They say now, you know, most people are gonna have two or three jobs, you know. So, I mean, things are changing.” This is hard for him to accept, since he very badly wants them to feel settled and financially stable, so they can start their families.

Now that he’s retired, Dave is getting into something of a routine of watching sports, walking the dogs, playing golf in the summer. But this life of leisure is punctuated with worry for his children.

I feel like especially now that I've lost my wife, there's not much that I want, you know what I mean? Like I don't, I don't want a big car, I don't want this or I don't want that. What I really want is my kids to be happy . . . I don't think my parents worried that much because I think life was easier for us and like I say, my career path was chosen by the time I was 25.

Dave is struck by the contrast to his own life at twenty-something, where economic and family stability went hand-in-hand with a consistent sense of purpose. He thinks of the factory job he held right out of university, which offered a good wage and job security, but no job satisfaction.

And you know, that's huge. I think, job satisfaction, like, my father-in-law says, “You're gonna spend one-third of your life at work, you might as well be happy with what you're doing.” And I've tried to instill that in my kids, like, you got to be happy with whatever you're doing, or otherwise, it's not worth doing. It’s hard to do something good, if you're not happy, or if you're not motivated.

For Dave, that motivation has been rooted in helping people and in “being the best, the best at what I did, and being really professional.” In speeches he gave as a Platoon Chief, Dave strived to pass this on to the younger generation: “I knew that when I put on that uniform for the first time, there were many, many men who had gone before me to give it the reputation that I had earned just by putting it on, and that I never want to do anything to tarnish that reputation, only something to enhance it.”

Still, there are still days when Dave is “back in that driveway of that house where the three kids died.” He finds it too emotional to visit the fire station. While he used to wonder why retirees didn’t come around, now he understands. “It’s almost like you lose something when you leave. And it’s hard to go back. It’s hard to explain.”
