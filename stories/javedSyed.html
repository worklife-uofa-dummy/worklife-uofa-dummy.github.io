Javed’s Story

With experienced precision, Javed manoeuvres his bus into its designated parking space in the coach yard. The vehicle settles into its stall as he turns off the engine. “This is stall number 85, home for my bus. She lives here.” Javed drives a coach in Fort McMurray, regularly ferrying 50 people a trip between the city and the nearby oil sands work sites. He has been with his company, Diversified, since 2008, earning him the 11th spot in seniority out of 180 employees in his division. Working hard ensures that he is hardly ever bothered by those above him, giving him an independence in his work that he appreciates. “Nobody ever asked me since the first day, what you are doing, why you’re doing [this]? Why you’re not doing this?…I don’t need any supervision on this. So I stuck with this.”

Javed started driving buses relatively late in life. Before moving to Canada, he had worked as an engineer in his home country of Pakistan as well as abroad. His decision to emigrate was made out of a desire to give his family opportunities that they wouldn’t have had at home. But after landing in Canada in the early 1990s, Javed faced a hard path. The struggling economy didn’t help. He was often not able to find work related to his training in engineering, sometimes for years at a time. The need to support his family meant taking the jobs he could get when he could get them. Providing for his family is still his main motivation, but it is frustrating that, in Fort McMurray, this means adjusting his life “around my work.”

While his current job as a coach driver is a far cry from the career he initially desired, Javed takes pride in the ability to apply his engineering skills to the task of shuttling his charges safely to and from work. In the often adverse road conditions of northern Alberta–especially in harsh winters–Javed thrives on the challenge. He enjoys knowing that his expertise with his bus means that his passengers can count on him:

As a young man, Javed studied mechanical engineering in Lahore, Pakistan. Shortly after graduating, in 1978, he attained an apprenticeship in the textile industry, where he spent a year learning how to apply his knowledge on actual machinery. Javed’s uncle then offered him an opportunity to work abroad. “He had a Human Resources Agency, supplying workforce to the Middle East, and I went to visit him and he said ‘you want to go to Iraq’? I said, ‘sure.’ He said, okay, bring me your resume and I’ll, I’ll send you out.” The Iraqi government was constructing a new airport in Baghdad, and Javed was given the task of lubricating heavy gravel haulers.

This massive infrastructure project required a large foreign labour force, but few considerations were made for the comfort of the workers. Javed recalls how harrowing it was to work outside in the sweltering heat:

I went there, it was crazy. For six months, I was under the sun, no shade on your head and it was 55 degrees outside. There was no place to even sit during your break, just your hat, and under your hat you are in the sun. I tell you, a human being can’t take it. During [my] one hour break, I used to sleep in the sun in 55 degrees. Never knowing where I am.

The outdoor environment proved so hostile that when Javed happened upon a job posting at the camp mess seeking workers with typing skills, he jumped at the opportunity. Moving into an inside work environment, he performed data entry for a French company. As this was still an early era for computing, the IBM machines that Javed worked with took up entire rooms of space, and data was entered on physical punch cards.

During his time at the French company, Javed’s boss encouraged him to study computer coding. Serendipitously, he was able to use this new knowledge to his advantage when the company suddenly found itself needing to calculate the payroll for 4000 employees (the manager who usually did this was away). Javed speaks with pride regarding the achievement: “[I said,] ‘I can do it. Just give me this data. Get the girls to do the punching, I will give you the salary tomorrow morning before people come to work.’ I got the job done before eight o’clock in the morning.” Following his success during this crisis, he was promoted to the role of computer operator.

Javed remembers his work at the French company fondly. He had earned the respect of his co-workers and found enjoyment in learning a new skill set. Even the outbreak of war between Iraq and Iran in 1980 didn’t lessen his fondness for his time working in Baghdad. He speaks glibly about his experience working during the war:

Two years, three years work[ing] there was nice. Iran was a good enemy. They don't bomb the public places, right? They know this is a runway we need to destroy. But this office beside it [where I worked], “We shouldn't bomb this one.” They were respectful enemies, eh?

In 1983, Javed returned to Pakistan. His relatives back home had facilitated an arranged marriage, and work abroad became less tenable in the midst of starting a family. Even with his experience, though, Javed had difficulty finding a way into the developing computing industry. He lacked the credentials that employers were looking for in potential candidates: “I was pretty good in the IBM mainframe. And there were only three [computer] systems at that time in my city. But they don’t let me even walk in, they need a Master’s in mathematics for that. That was the minimum requirement.” While he was disappointed, Javed was able to fall back on his mechanical engineering education, obtaining a job as a production engineering designer for Samsonite luggage.

During his time at Samsonite, Javed and his wife had two sons. The boys’ arrival changed the calculus of Javed’s work life. As his children grew up, the small town in which the family lived didn’t offer the opportunities Javed wishes for his sons to have. Javed resigned his position with Samsonite in favour of a job with a deep freeze manufacturer in Lahore, with closer access to good education for his sons.

As he tells it, Javed’s decision to emigrate to Canada several years later, in 1991, was also motivated by his children:

It wasn't a very big thing. I had a good job. And to be an engineer for a big company [in Pakistan] : you have a chauffeur driven car, with a uniform…and everything. But one day, my son came from school and he said, “Daddy, I want to go to Disneyland.” I said, “What do you mean, why?”...And he said, “My class fellows are going. Summer vacation is coming and they are going to see Disneyland.” And okay, [I think to myself,] I have a good job and everything, but I can't take them to Disneyland with this job. So I thought, I have to do something. So bringing my son to Disneyland brought me to Canada.

Javed says he had very little knowledge of Canada before he emigrated. “There was no internet at the time,” he points out, “so basically, I knew Montreal, because the Pakistani [field] hockey team won an Olympic medal there. I think it was the ‘76 Montreal Olympics.” While relocating to the United States was another option that he and his family considered, the liberal immigration laws of Canada in the 1990s made it the more appealing choice. “I think it was kind of a little bit open policy of Canada.”

Unfortunately, Javed once again found himself struggling to find work related to his engineering background and experience. The Canadian economy was in a recession, making jobs harder to come by. For three years, Javed drove a cab in Toronto to make ends meet. He would pick up other odd jobs in factories and as temp labour until 1995, when he was hired to a new permanent position as a production manager. There, he found some unexpected connections to his previous work: “It was making automotive parts. The plastic and my Samsonite experience counted there, right? They were exactly the same machines, Italian machines, which I was working on in Pakistan.” This new job allowed Javed and his family to maintain a relatively stable income for the next decade.

Just as with his arrival in Canada in the early 1990s, the next big shift in Javed’s career would come at a time of economic recession. When the 2008 financial crisis hit, Javed arrived at work one day to find the business had been locked up overnight. Javed remembers the feeling of fear that came with sudden unemployment:

I don’t have a job. I tried to find another job and they were paying like, what, half of what I was expecting, at least half. And they were too far. I was living right in Toronto, east end. And most of the jobs were in the west end, or around Niagara Falls, Hamilton area. That far.

Afraid that he wouldn’t be able to provide for his family, he began looking at the possibility of uprooting once more in order to find secure employment. A friend in Fort McMurray messaged Javed to tell him that there were plenty of job opportunities in the oil sands for those with an upgraded licence. Following a quick training course, certifying him to drive buses, Javed drove all the way from Toronto to Fort McMurray: “I filled [my van] up with stuff and told my wife and kids, ‘Okay, I’ll get a job. I’ll call you guys.’ Stayed with that friend for a few days. The next morning he brought me to Diversified, and they were real short on drivers at that time.”

Javed was immediately hired by the company and since then he has ferried workers from the town of Fort McMurray out to their work shifts in the surrounding oil sands. Ensuring that his passengers get to and from work safely is his top priority, and in 14 years on the job, he confidently points out that he has had no safety incidents. Javed attributes his strong safety record to the fact that he is able to apply the knowledge and skills of his career in engineering to the act of driving. It gives him a perspective on his job that not every other driver has. As he’s driving he asks himself: “The load, what is the load? If I’m making a turn, what is the centrifugal force? What is the centripetal force? How fast I can make this turn?”

The challenge of safe driving in adverse weather makes the job satisfying for Javed:

This is a very challenging job, especially in Fort McMurray. From here to go to Suncor on a regular basis takes me about 40 minutes. I have done this trip in six hours, when the weather is bad...because it was so much ice, inch by inch, inch by inch. I have my tires on the rumble strip, you know, to get some traction. Nobody [else] was coming down, there was trailers crookedly stalled in the middle of the hill. And I came down with 50 people in my bus and brought them home and found out I love challenges! I can do things differently. Sometimes you can’t see nothing and you have to bring these 50 people to work and home. It’s challenging. And I like it. I love driving in snow.

Being able to do his job skillfully also ensures that Javed is afforded a freedom and independence that he finds comforting. This is one of the things that initially struck him when he first obtained the job back in 2008. “I was so, so relaxed in this job,” he explains. “You need punctuality, you need a certain kind of mindset for this job. And after that, nobody is sitting on your shoulder. You are free to do things the way you like it…And I found it comforting, you know?”

Javed is always looking to improve his service to his passengers: “Last year, I told my manager who was interviewing me, I said, now my priorities are serving my customers better.” Unfortunately, Javed’s passengers don’t always return his positive sentiments, sometimes treating him disrespectfully. “These are the people who don’t realise that they’re making lots of money. That changes their attitude. They don’t give a damn who you are.” He notes that with the job structure of a resource town like Fort McMurray there comes a shift in the class structure. “That’s one of the basic issues with the city. That we have two tiers, two categories of citizenship in the city. And one don’t like to talk to the other. That’s the problem.” Workers who are directly employed by big energy and resource extraction companies often receive greater pay and better benefits than workers with identical jobs outside that sphere. This frustrates Javed: “Same driver [working out at site] is making $60 an hour and [another driver,] like me, $30 an hour.”

The gap between these tiers of workers leads to friction between the two groups. With the cost of living constantly increasing in Fort McMurray, Javed regularly has to work extra hours to keep up.

I'm working, like, 21 days to get my week off. And then I still work three extra days…to make a little bit more money and be competitive in this city, financially…Otherwise I'm not compatible with [the other class of workers]. The lifestyle they live and the lifestyle I live, there can’t be any comparison if I don't work extra.

For all the confidence and pride that Javed has in what he does, he feels reluctant to admit what he does to his family back home. “Where I come from, and with my educational background, even my sister doesn’t know what I do. I know if she knows, she will kill herself just crying, ‘My brother’s driving a bus!’…Because back home, it’s just illiterate people who can’t do nothing.” He compares his circumstances to his sister’s, who employs a cook and two live-in servants. “Since I came to Canada, she [has] always asked me a question. She says, ‘Big brother, what do you have in Canada, which I don’t have here in Pakistan?’ And I have no answer.”

In this situation, disruptions to work can threaten Javed’s family’s monetary security. When the COVID pandemic hit, he was already in the midst of a personal health crisis, having developed a blood clot that moved to his lungs. The recovery time for his sudden illness had caused him to miss a great deal of work. When he did return to driving bus, still during the pandemic, he was sometimes frustrated by the unfair impacts on safety and pay:

I didn't work that much in the beginning of COVID. I came back and…now we are taking 36 passengers [per bus instead of 50]. So the company needs more buses to cover the same run. So more buses mean more money for the company, while I'm getting the same pay. If I'm sitting here [in the driver’s seat], 26 people are passing by me with the virus. Nobody's paying me extra for that.

There is one small silver lining to the timing of the pandemic: it gave Javed an unexpected financial reprieve after being off work for so long due to his blood clot. “COVID was good for me in a sense that, when I got sick, after a week this thing started. And after a few weeks, I got a call from my bank. If you can’t afford to pay your mortgage, because of COVID we can defer it.”

At 62, Javed knows that he won’t be able to work forever. But due to the financial constraints on him and his wife, he feels that he is a long way from being secure enough to retire. The end of the oil boom has meant that the house that they bought in Fort McMurray soon after their arrival is now worth much less than it was at the time of purchase. With little invested in his retirement, he remains dependent on his work to support his family, even as it has meant sacrificing other important parts of his life:

One aspect of life Javed makes sure to fit in around his work is photography. He especially likes photographing wildlife and family events, and has had some of his images published. This lifelong hobby, launched when his uncle gave him a Diana camera when Javed was a teenager, brings him a sense of freedom that Javed wants to preserve. “I never tried to make money out of it. I know if somebody started paying for this, then it will be a burden on my mind.”

For now, Java hopes he can count on steady income as a driver. He points out that one major advantage of driving a bus for a living is that he can rely on being able to do it even as he ages: “I can keep it as long as I can walk. And I like to, I want to get up every day to have something to do. If it’s not taking out the dog, at least I can take, you know, 50 people to work.”
