Cindy’s Story

As Cindy reflects on how she ended up coming to Canada as a live-in caregiver, she says, “Everywhere I go, I always am a member of a choir.” This comes to mind because it was on her way to a choir event, on a train in Hong Kong, that Cindy encountered a woman who encouraged her to apply to work in Canada. Several months later, with her Labor Market Impact Assessment and visa in hand, she landed in Fort McMurray, where a booming oil economy had led to a boom in paid care work: “February the second I’m here, 2006. Yes.” Her daughters and husband, whom she had already been away from for eleven years as an overseas worker, balked at the additional time apart. Cindy responded, “But think of the result of this. After two years, I can actually process your papers and you could come to Canada and stay with me.”

Sustaining her family, despite the pain of distance, is a continuous thread in Cindy’s migratory work life:

[Being away from family], always it's hard. It's real. It's like in the real sense of the word. But then you also have that drive to continue doing what you're doing because you want the best for them. You want to give them the best life that they could have. I send them money every month of my life from Singapore to Hong Kong to Canada. Never missed a month that I don’t send them money.

Alongside this drive is a deep sense of making and building community connections wherever Cindy finds herself. Being a part of a choir, volunteering at a local family center, and supporting women as they transition out of being a nanny—just as she once did—are interwoven with her current paid work life as a childcare professional and cleaning business entrepreneur. They blend into a web of activity to which she gives her all.

It was her father, a farmer in the mountains of the southern Tagalog region of the Philippines, that advised Cindy on adapting and contributing wherever you land. He recognized early on that she would land on her feet, despite their poverty: “I came from a very poor family. When we were growing up, we don’t have anything like [the toys now], like we don’t even have TV. We don’t even have a road, all we have is a pathway.” It was thus a big move when Cindy was accepted to study for a college degree at a convent in Baguio City. There, she had her first formal job. As a work-study student, she made cookies from communion wafer leftovers:

I was thinking about a Bachelor of Science in Psychology. But anyway, I'm working as, what do you call this, what do I call me? So I'm a working student, and I'm doing mostly cookies. It’s made of the host cuttings . . . it was sold to people. And that's where we get our salary. And that's the salary we use as our payment for school.

Cindy was never able to finish her degree because in 1988, she got pregnant and had to leave school and get married.

It's not uncommon. You know, especially when you were living in a convent. You probably can imagine how it is, like very strict rules and stuff like that. But then we go out to the university, we met people. Yeah. And that's how I get my two daughters. So we get married then. And, yeah, I stopped, I stopped the school. Because I got pregnant. And then after that, I said, Okay, I will continue [my school] after, but never happened.

Without a college degree, Cindy did not have a lot of work options, so in 1990 she did what tens of thousands of Filipino women do around the world: she took a position as a domestic helper. The family she worked for was nice enough, but the work conditions in Singapore were strict and harsh, with only one day off a month, and curfews. Cindy likened this to prison but distinguished the systemic issues from the daily gratification of work.

I'm the kind of person who, whatever I do, I enjoy it. Like, the work that I did in Singapore, it's not that I don't like it, but I don't like the way it is being set up. You know, the rules and all that. But I, I give my 100% to every job that I do. Right now. So when you do that you enjoy it. And you, yeah, you put your mind and your whole self into it.

Cindy returned home after her two-year contract was up, and a year later she was pregnant with her second daughter. It was now her husband’s turn to do overseas work, so she stayed home with her two young children while he went to Taiwan. Unfortunately, his contract was cut short and they ended up falling back on Cindy’s savings from Singapore, which just wasn’t sustainable. The global demand for live-in caregivers made this the most reliable choice.

So, in 1997, when her second daughter was three years old, Cindy left for Hong Kong. There, she worked for the same family for nine years. She raised their young son, went to the market each day, cleaned, and handled the household budget. Like many other caregivers in Hong Kong, Cindy encountered disparaging remarks, such as being called guyong, or “maid,” out in public. But unlike them, she was relatively free to live her own life once her work for the day was done.

I came when my lady employer was still pregnant, two weeks before he came. So as soon as they get home from the hospital, they gave him to me. In my room. He stayed with me for nine years . . . so again, I was one of those lucky domestic helpers who found an employer that is really, really very good to me and, like, treat me as a family.

Cindy grew to love that young boy, but after that chance encounter on the train she didn’t feel she could pass up the promise of reuniting with her own family by taking a position in Canada.

Cindy’s plan was to work the required two years as a live-in caregiver and then find other work in Canada and sponsor her family to join her. Through connections she made in Fort McMurray during those two years, and with the help of a supportive employer, Cindy had a full-time job the moment she got the proper paperwork (an open work permit). It helped that her female employer “dragged” Cindy along to various volunteer activities, one of which led to paid work running the programming at the local HUB Family Resource Center.

Here, you can actually put [volunteering] in your resume, which is what I needed. So I got all those experiences, and one of my regular volunteer work was at The HUB. Even if I'm just a volunteer, I ran their programming. They let me do what they're doing. So they know me, they see how I work and how I treated them and stuff. [The Director] approached me then and said, ‘Whenever you get your papers, and you want to work with us, let me know, you have a job.’ So then I was thrilled of course, because I really want the work that they're doing there. And so then in 2008 I got my open work permit in October. And she's like, come over. And you can start on Monday. Just like that . . . Full time, right away.

The period from 2008 to 2014 was a complex mix of business success and family change for Cindy. Time away from her husband had taken its toll, and in 2010 Cindy finally decided to start divorce proceedings. She devoted all her energy to sponsoring her two daughters to join her after more than a dozen years apart. Cindy had saved enough from working at The HUB, plus doing part-time work as a house cleaner and restaurant server, to buy a house. All she could afford in the hot oil boom-driven housing market of Fort McMurray was an old and dirty “fixer upper.” But then “slowly, with renovation, it became now our house ever since.” With some resourcefulness she was able to fill the house with furniture left behind by some departing oil workers. For Cindy, the house was full of both material and symbolic value, especially given all that had been sacrificed:

I don't want my kids to come to Canada and they will be sharing a room and they will fight—you know, how siblings do—and they cannot do what they used to do. And they will tell me ‘Oh, I should have stayed in the Philippines. It's better in the Philippines than here.’ I don't want them to have that reason.

In 2012, her daughters—now in their late teens and early twenties—finally arrived.

These weren’t the only new work-life events during this time, as Cindy found herself entering the world of commercial cleaning. While cleaning offices at night for someone else, she happened to also overhear some people talking about setting up a new company and thought, “Why not set up my own cleaning company?” In 2010 she registered Cindyrella cleaning, and through word of mouth, the business steadily grew. By 2014 she had to quit working at The HUB. About the same time, an acquaintance who trusted her skill and instinct begged her to help set up their dry cleaning business, Enviro Clean. Cindy balked at first, but before she knew it she was learning a new set of skills.

I said, ‘Oh, I know nothing about dry cleaning [and] honestly, I just put in my resignation to focus on my business.’ But then he's like, please, please, you can also do your business while working with us. All we want you to do is just manage the staff, hiring the staff and all those kinds of things and we don't know anybody else who could do some things like that. And they offered me a good deal [with bonuses] . . . And they send me to all these trainings, sent me to the U.S. and I learned everything from scratch.

Cindy had planned on managing Enviro Clean for just one year, given the demands of her own Cindyrella commercial cleaning business, but somehow, one year stretched into six. Across this time, as economic and environmental ups and downs in Fort McMurray took their toll, Cindy experienced a growing desire to leave Enviro Clean. In 2015, a downturn in oil prices started to eat away at business. Then came the cleanup from the spring 2016 wildfire; after evacuation and a month away, she came back to “almost 24 hours we’re running. I had people working at night, folding, sorting out clothes. Oh my god. Clothes everywhere.” Then in April 2020, just after the onset of COVID, downtown Fort McMurray experienced a “100-year flood.” One of the big buildings Cindyrella serviced was so badly damaged that the contract was no longer needed. Finally, in summer 2020, COVID layoffs and loss of business at Enviro Clean gave Cindy the “opportunity to exit” and concentrate on Cindyrella.

COVID posed its own ups and downs for Cindyrella’s commercial cleaning contracts. Early on there was a surge of new business, especially for disinfecting. She and her staff had to learn about the proper products and procedures for this work, but more than this, it posed possible exposure to the virus:

Especially whenever there is a positive case in the workplace, they will ask me to do a disinfection during the night. Of course, we are all fully geared to protect ourselves. But most of the time I’m the first one in the space because I don't want my workers to think that I’m exposing them to the virus first.

Then came a downturn as more people were working from home. Cindy’s clients reduced the frequency of cleaning and asked her to reduce prices given their own loss of business—the domino effect of COVID and work. And even when Cindy did have contracts, keeping staff was hard. She was grateful when she could hire Abby [PARTICIPANT], another former caregiver, to manage the paperwork for Cindyrella.

Cindy is glad she left Enviro Clean to concentrate on Cindyrella, but she misses the salary. The extra cash would be especially welcome given the drastic devaluation of her house since the 2015 downturn. There’s also the cost of further renovations: Cindy’s house was also severely damaged in the April 2020 floods. The upside of quitting Enviro Clean, for Cindy, is her return to work at The HUB Family Resource Centre. Throughout the years, whenever they saw her, they would ask “Are you coming back?” Cindy had always said “Not yet,” but in 2021 she felt she could finally say yes to working there part-time.

Cindy laughs, saying “I cannot get away from The HUB!” She muses that it has probably become such a constant thread in her work life because working with children and parents through music, parenting classes, and child development programming is her joy. But it is the music program that stands out:

It’s separate from what’s going on in their life every day . . . There’s like dads who come and you can see them doing the chicken dance and doing silly things. And it gives me so much satisfaction seeing them like that.

It is also through The HUB that Cindy has supported and managed the Nanny Network, an organization that supports live-in caregivers. Having run or co-run the Network since 2008, she talks about how dear it is to her: “I know how it feels to be a nanny. I know the challenges that they go through,” ranging from daunting immigration paperwork to everyday racist encounters.

It is not always clear where work ends and life begins for Cindy. Supporting and hiring former caregivers, organizing events for the Filipino community, and retaining social networks with former employers blur the lines. But it is clear that work is for family and community. Cindy now has a new partner, is a grandmother, and is re-establishing relations with her LGBTQ daughter. Her children remind her that sometimes Cindy is still trying too hard to make up for lost time:

Even though my kids are grown up, I still feel that I still have that feeling that I, I want to give everything that I could for them. And if they needed help, then I have something to give them. You cover it with everything that you can give. And then when you say no to them, you [worry that] you're a bad mom. You're this, you’re that . . . But it's so hard to explain why you left them that early and stayed away for so long with them. So, so hard.

Cindy tells her daughters of the importance of working for themselves, not to support her.

So, another important motivator for working hard is that when she “cannot do the job anymore,” she won’t be dependent on her daughters. Cindy is aiming to retire by the age of 60, with the devaluation of her house being a big potential barrier. She can imagine staying in Fort McMurray, but going somewhere warm each winter.
