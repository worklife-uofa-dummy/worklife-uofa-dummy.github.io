Abella’s Story

“I was born, really, as a hardworking person,” says Abby. “If you’re a working person, your body, you know, it’s something that your body wants to do? It’s something that you’re missing . . . I mean, you miss earning, you want to make money.” This material pull to work—both bodily and monetary—is palpable as Abby contemplates how long she will stay on maternity leave. Going back to work full-time as a bookkeeper and housekeeper is not necessarily possible, given ongoing pain from postpartum complications and COVID job disruptions, but the arrival of her husband and children from the Philippines just over a year ago makes it almost imperative. Besides, Abby has often worked in and through difficult conditions, from supporting her poor family as the oldest of seven children to working in harsh conditions as an overseas factory worker in Taiwan. When asked what advice she might give to her ten-year-old self about her future as a working person, Abby says “I mean, work hard. Always think of family for inspiration . . . no matter what hindrance or struggle, stay strong.”

The small barrio in which Abby grew up was “not a very civilized place,” with jeepneys and tricycles for transportation. She remembers her family being looked down upon for their relatively poor house and living conditions. Abby worked from a very young age, helping with her aunt’s and mother’s home-based businesses. She packaged uniforms and coffee filters and made paper bags for packing rice.

I never miss a year without having a job, going from any kind of work to any kind of work. It is the way that we can buy our own things for school, and some things to help my mom and dad because back then it’s not easy, like life, for us. I think I start working since a child . . . I think I was five or six, six years old. So that's where we used to do.

In high school Abby’s mother bought two sewing machines and taught her and her sister how to sew so they could be part of the dressmaking business. All of this work helped pay for college, where Abby worked part-time in a print shop, typing and editing things like calling cards and invitations.

Her goal throughout was to eventually go abroad to the US, Europe, or Canada. This goal was inspired by other overseas workers in her family, including her own father, but also by the discrimination she saw in the Filipino job market and that she experienced growing up in poverty—“so I didn’t put myself down but instead made it like a tool to be inspired and work hard.” Once she graduated from college in computer administration, and as her country and family faced a financial crisis, she started the process.

Abby knew that getting to one of the western countries required previous experience, so she took a “stepping stone” job in Taiwan in the early 2000s, where she worked for three years in a CD factory “working like a machine nonstop.” She and the other foreign workers lived in a dirty dormitory, “like a military setting.” Despite early experiences of racist mistreatment, and being told she was slow, Abby proved herself and eventually moved up to assistant supervisor. Almost all of her earnings went back to her mother and sisters in the Philippines.

Once back in the Philippines Abby worked as an executive assistant and then in various government jobs, finally able to use some of the skills from her college degree. But things soon changed again in 2008, when Abby got married and moved to the village where her husband lived and worked as a butcher. With one child and another one on the way, her husband headed to the Middle East to build oil tanks. Abby cared for her two young children and once again worked from home, this time selling delicacies online. When he returned a couple of years later, she could once again take a government job.

Then in 2015, Abby found herself back overseas, helping care for her sister’s baby in Japan. This inspired her to think again about getting to someplace like Canada, so Abby returned to the Philippines six months later to enrolled in the preparation program for overseas caregiving work. Her cousin was already in Fort McMurray as a live-in caregiver and recommended Abby as a replacement when the contract with her employer had finished. Denied on her first try, Abby applied again, paid the application fee a second time, and was finally accepted to go to Canada. The goal this time was to get permanent residency status and sponsor her family to join her.

Leaving her own children to work abroad was very different than going as a single person, but then, they were also her very reason for leaving:

When Abby first arrived in Fort McMurray in early 2017, it took her a while to realize that she didn’t have to go the extra mile to prove that her employer “can trust me and know that I am worthy.”

They always tell me ‘Don't work too hard.’ And I say ‘No, it's fine. I can do this.’ So yeah, I have to prove them that it’s worth it that they bring me here . . . So I have to prove them. And then they saw my hard working and that's why, yeah, they love me.

Looking at her first photos from 2019, Abby says she misses that time. It was her and her family’s last big stepping stone, and she wasn’t just a caregiver “but a member of the family.” She is “forever grateful” because if it weren’t for her employer she would not be here. She doesn’t miss shoveling snow, but remembers fondly the routine of preparing the kids’ breakfasts and school lunches, feeding the dog, learning to drive, and generally doing things to give “in return what they have given to me.” She points out that their jobs weren’t easy, leaving for their shifts out at oil sands sites at 4 o’clock in the morning and coming home tired at 7 or 8 p.m.

Even after her employers changed jobs and were home more, they kept Abby on full time. She was grateful for this, given the need to finish out her two-year contract if she was to apply for permanent residency (PR) and sponsor her family, and the money and time that this required. There was the English proficiency exam, a credential assessment, gathering police clearances from other countries, and most difficult of all, constant changes in the required paperwork. Abby had to re-do her application three times, with little to fall back on. During her two-year absence, Abby’s husband had only been able to work-part-time while caring for the children.

Once she had her open work permit, and while waiting for PR status and family sponsorship, Abby began working as a housekeeper and bookkeeper for her friend’s cleaning company. She remembers this transition time as full of mixed emotions: excitement about welcoming her family, anxiety about having funding and paperwork in hand, and sadness “because you will leave behind this family that you’ve been true to for two years.” On top of this, Abby had to evacuate the house in which she was living during the big flood of April 2020, and it was just one month before her family arrived that she was able to secure an apartment through Wood Buffalo Housing. The COVID pandemic had started, which meant “delays, delays, delayed in everything” for her family’s arrival. Finally, in June 2020, they landed in Fort McMurray.

COVID was hard. After an initial surge in business, the office cleaning company lost half of its business as clients sought to cut costs and people worked from home. Cindy took CERB for about six months but then in March 2021, once it was considered taxable, she thought “Okay, let’s stop this. We can still manage with the finances we have.” Her husband was able to find a job at Walmart, albeit with a disappointing paycheque. Pregnant with their third child and unable to work as much due to COVID, Abby decided to take an online course to build her administration skills, “doing something to progress myself.”

A family in Fort McMurray asked if she would care for their children, and while the income during COVID times was tempting, ultimately Abby decided she didn’t need or want it: “It’s not like it used to be. I mean, I live here, I can stay here as long as I want. . . And I have my own kids as well that I have to take care of so maybe I can’t be a caregiver anymore.” She worries for the future of the live-in caregiver program, given the new $10/day childcare program in the province. Then she thinks again: perhaps nannies will be preferred because of the extra chores they do!

Not being able to support family back home during COVID has been really difficult. Abby’s aging parents both contracted the coronavirus and faced high medical bills, and most of her siblings lost their jobs. They had enough food because they could continue to plant, but they have been asking her for help. “Before I could help, but now, I’m sorry, I can’t help.” And then when Abby’s father died of COVID in late 2020 it was impossible to get home to her family. “It’s so devastating. Like, for me and my sister in Japan, we were not able to go home.”

Overall, Abby feels that “you can put me anywhere” and she will find a way to enjoy her work. In Taiwan, the work was hard but gratifying “because of the people that you are with.” Likewise, Abby has enjoyed cleaning alongside other Filipina-Canadian women in Fort McMurray:

Work is not only enjoyable, but a kind of compulsion driven by family, survival, and social responsibility.

I work because for a living, we work for a living? I mean, you work because you have to live. Because if you don’t work, I mean, what's the purpose? I mean, you work to provide for your family. Of course, that's your aim. I'm working because I have the need of this, because I have to work for my family. Like especially for the future of my kids. Yeah, I work because I have to contribute on the society. I feel I have something to contribute to the society or to the company that I work in. So, everything.

Abby hopes that with new coursework under her belt, she can once again work for a school or for the municipality—or maybe even as an administrator out at an oil sands site. She hopes her family can stay in Fort McMurray “because I love it here . . . I tend to be loyal, like, this is the place that gave me opportunities, it is the place where I will render my service, pay back like in the community.” Abby feels similarly toward Canada more generally and feels good about paying taxes to do her part.

Just as Abby has been able to raise the standard of living for her family back in the Philippines, where they now have a presentable house, she hopes her three children will do well.

I hope they can graduate college. I hope we can, you know, provide for them and for whatever career they choose . . . Life must go on and keep pushing and pushing. We have this saying in Tagalog that means that in living life, there’s always hope.
